# CtrlS — The Growth That Stopped At The Interest Line

> Two ICRA rating rationales on CtrlS Datacenters, seven months apart, tell one story each and a third when read together. The first, September 2025, describes a company adding 40–45 MW a year, every megawatt pre-leased, on ₹4,500–4,600 Cr of capex across FY26–FY28. The second, May 2026, describes **₹13,000–13,500 Cr across FY27–FY28 alone** — 2.91× the capital in two-thirds the time — chasing **950–1,000 MW by FY2029** against 150 MW today. That is a **6.47× step-up in required build rate**, decided in seven months. What both documents also show is what the *previous, smaller* plan already did: FY24 to FY25, revenue grew 16.65%, margin expanded 2.9 points, operating EBITDA grew **23.90%** — and **profit after tax did not move at all**, ₹248 Cr to ₹248 Cr. Interest coverage fell 5.3× to 3.9×, and roughly **54% of the entire EBITDA increase went to interest.** The pre-leasing is real and it removes demand risk, which is more than either of the last two case studies could say. It also hands the specification, the price and the roadmap to whoever absorbs the capacity.

## 1. Executive Summary

CtrlS is India's largest Tier-4 colocation provider: **17 operational data centres across 8 cities, 7 more under construction, 150 MW of operational IT capacity** as at FY2026. 🟢 ICRA upgraded it to **[ICRA]AA (Stable)** in September 2025 and reaffirmed in May 2026. In August 2026 it raised **₹250 Cr**, of which **₹200 Cr from Nikhil Kamath**, and it is reported to be targeting a **~$300 Mn IPO by FY27**. 🟡

The operating business is genuinely good. Operating income went **₹1,339 Cr (FY24) → ₹1,562 Cr (FY25) → ₹1,900 Cr (FY26E)**, with OPBDITA margin expanding **46.7% → 49.6% → 50–52%**. 🟢 A ~50% EBITDA margin is a real asset and **2.25× the margin of the only listed Indian comparator** (§14).

Three disclosed facts complicate the picture.

**Growth stopped at the interest line.** Between FY24 and FY25, revenue grew 16.65% and OPBDITA grew **23.90%** — an increase of **₹149.4 Cr** — while **PAT stayed at ₹248 Cr, growth of exactly zero.** Interest coverage fell from **5.3× to 3.9×**, which implies interest expense rising from ~₹118.0 Cr to ~₹198.7 Cr: **₹80.7 Cr, or 53.98% of the entire EBITDA increase.** 🟢

**The plan then got much bigger.** Between the two rationales, capex guidance went from ₹4,550 Cr over three years to **₹13,250 Cr over two** — **4.37× the annual run-rate** — and the capacity ambition from 40–45 MW a year to **950–1,000 MW by FY2029**, a required build rate of **275 MW/year against a demonstrated 42.5**. The company has shown it can build **15.45%** of the rate its own plan now needs. 🟢

**And the arithmetic the plan implies is severe.** At 60–65% debt funding, the FY27–FY28 capex adds roughly **₹8,281 Cr of new debt** to an implied **₹2,944 Cr** already outstanding. To hold leverage at the 4× ICRA flags as the boundary, OPBDITA would have to reach **₹2,806 Cr — 2.90× the FY26E level** — implying revenue of about **₹5,503 Cr** against ₹1,900 Cr today. Undrawn facilities of ₹4,665 Cr cover **56.33%** of the required new debt. 🟢

This is not a demand problem. ICRA states that **"entire incremental large capacities are pre-leased with a hyperscaler."** 🟢 That is the right structure and the opposite of Day 60 and Day 61. But it relocates the risk rather than removing it: a colocation provider whose incremental capacity is fully pre-leased to hyperscalers is no longer running a multi-tenant product — it is a **balance-sheet-financed build-to-suit contractor**, and the counterparty writing the lease writes the specification.

The proposal is **CtrlS Regulated Capacity**: a published, lender-visible floor reserving a defined share of every new build for regulated Indian workloads at enterprise pricing. North Star **EMW/100 — Enterprise Megawatts per 100 MW commissioned**, denominated in *commissioned* capacity so a hyperscaler-only building lowers it. Guardrail **VUR-90 — Vacant Reserved-capacity Rate at the 90th percentile**, because a reserve that never fills is value destruction.

Under a stress test built from CtrlS's own two documents — **15.45%**, demonstrated build rate over required build rate — the proposal falls from 3rd of 4 to **last**, behind a power-procurement programme this case study did not propose.

## 3. Why This Product, Why Now

B2B infrastructure was the last untouched domain in this series, and data centres are the most capital-intensive product category in Indian technology right now.

The specific reason for today is that **two rating rationales seven months apart give a controlled before-and-after** on the same company, written by the same analyst house against the same methodology. That is rare. It means the change in plan can be measured rather than inferred, and it means the FY24–FY25 outcome can be read as evidence about what the *smaller* version of the plan already cost.

It also completes an unplanned three-day arc on capital and demand. Day 60: IndiGo, a cost base growing regardless of capacity, with no forward demand at all. Day 61: Atomberg, buying factories with nothing contracted. Day 62: CtrlS, the most capital-hungry of the three — **and the only one that pre-sells.**

## 5. Product Overview

CtrlS sells colocation: rack space, power, cooling, connectivity and physical security, under medium-to-long-term contracts, across **17 operational data centres in 8 cities with 150 MW of operational IT capacity**. 🟢 Its differentiation is Tier-4 certification — the highest uptime tier — which matters to regulated buyers and matters much less to a hyperscaler that builds its own redundancy in software.

---

## 6. History & Timeline

Founded 2008 in Hyderabad by Sridhar Pinnapureddy, CtrlS built its position on Tier-4 certification and enterprise trust rather than scale. Capacity went **87 MW (March 2024) → 130 MW (August 2025) → 150 MW (FY2026)**; ICRA upgraded the rating to AA in September 2025 and enhanced the rated amount from ₹2,500 Cr to **₹3,700 Cr, a 48% increase**. 🟢

---

## 7. Market Context

Indian data centre demand is being reshaped by hyperscaler AI capacity, which arrives in units of tens of megawatts and on build-to-suit terms rather than rack-by-rack. ICRA names **"stiff competition from large DC addition with entry of many new players"** as a key risk 🟢 — the category is attracting capital faster than it is attracting differentiated demand, which is what usually precedes a pricing problem.

---

## 8. Regulatory & Policy Context

Two regulatory forces pull in opposite directions, and the proposal in §50 lives between them.

**Data localisation creates the enterprise demand.** RBI's payment-data localisation mandate, sectoral requirements across BFSI and insurance, and the DPDP framework now being operationalised all require certain Indian data to sit in India, under auditable control, with named accountability. That demand is regulatory in origin, price-insensitive relative to hyperscale, and it is precisely what a Tier-4 multi-tenant facility is built to serve.

**Capital policy creates the hyperscale demand.** Data centres have been granted infrastructure status and various state-level power, land and duty incentives, which is what makes ₹13,250 Cr of two-year capex financeable at all.

The tension is that both demands compete for the **same commissioned megawatts**, and only one of them signs a pre-lease for an entire building before the concrete is poured. A firm that funds its build on pre-leases will, absent a deliberate constraint, allocate every new megawatt to the counterparty that pre-leases — which is rational per building and, compounded, changes what the company is.

---

## 9. Problem Statement

**For the business:** CtrlS must roughly triple OPBDITA merely to hold leverage at 4× after a capex programme it has not yet demonstrated it can execute at the required rate. The financing works only if the capacity lands roughly on time and the leases hold.

**For the product organisation:** when incremental capacity is fully pre-leased to hyperscalers, there is no capacity left to sell to the enterprise buyers whose regulatory need is growing and whose contracts carry the margin and the stickiness. The product roadmap becomes whatever the pre-lease specifies.

---

## 10. Who Actually Pays

ICRA discloses that the **top 10 customers contributed ~62% of consolidated operating income in FY2025** — about **₹968.4 Cr**, averaging **₹96.8 Cr each** — leaving **₹593.6 Cr** across everyone else. 🟢 At FY26E revenue over 150 MW, the business earns roughly **₹12.67 Cr per operational megawatt**, which is the single most useful unit for everything that follows.

---

## 11. Customer Personas

*Constructed from ICRA's disclosed client mix, not primary research — see ASSUMPTIONS Part 3.*

**The hyperscaler** takes whole buildings, pre-leases before construction, sets the specification, and is credit-perfect — but buys on price per megawatt and provides its own resilience. **The regulated enterprise** (BFSI, insurance, healthcare) takes racks not buildings, pays a premium for Tier-4 and audit support, stays for years, and is the reason CtrlS's margin is what it is. **The digital-native scale-up** sits between the two and converts to whichever gets capacity first.

---

## 12. Customer Journey

For the enterprise the journey is a long qualification — security audit, compliance review, migration plan — followed by a contract measured in years and very low churn. For the hyperscaler it is a capacity negotiation that begins before the building exists and ends in a pre-lease.

The two journeys compete for one inventory, and only the second one is visible when a financing decision is being made.

## 13. TAM / SAM / SOM — Restricted Form

*Framework note: restricted form, sized from the company's own disclosed base. No primary-sourced Indian data-centre market size could be verified to this series' standard (§63); the default since Day 57.*

The disclosed base is **150 MW of operational capacity generating ₹1,900 Cr (FY26E)** — **₹12.67 Cr per MW**. The disclosed ambition is **950–1,000 MW by FY2029**; at today's revenue per megawatt that would be roughly **₹12,350 Cr of revenue**, which is the number the capex plan is implicitly underwritten against.

**That extrapolation is almost certainly too generous, and the reason is the case study's point.** Hyperscale megawatts do not earn enterprise revenue per megawatt — they are sold wholesale, in bulk, to a counterparty with alternatives. Applying a blended historical figure to a book that is shifting toward pre-leased hyperscale overstates the outcome, and by how much is Assumption A4, which §53 measures first.

## 14. Competitive Landscape — Restricted To Filers

*Framework note: restricted to competitors whose numbers are filed. **Sify Technologies is NASDAQ-listed and files with the SEC**, and reported FY2025-26 in April 2026. **Nxtra is consolidated inside Bharti Airtel and Anant Raj's data centre business sits inside a listed parent — both file, neither is separable to a standard this series accepts, so both are named and neither is deep-dived.** CtrlS's own figures come from ICRA rationales, not from CtrlS.*

**Sify, FY2025-26: revenue ₹4,487.7 Cr (+12.51%)**, of which **Data Centre Services ₹1,751.9 Cr (+23%)**, 39% of the total. **EBITDA ₹987.1 Cr, up 30.53%.** And **net loss ₹136.6 Cr, widened 74.01%** from ₹78.5 Cr. Capex ₹1,328.2 Cr; net debt ₹3,353.4 Cr. Capacity: **129 MW cumulative sold, 17 MW sold in FY26, 81 MW contracted for FY27** — **4.76× the FY26 rate.** 🟢

Three readings.

**CtrlS runs the better business.** Its 49.6% OPBDITA margin is **2.25×** Sify's 22.0% EBITDA margin. Colocation done well is a high-margin business and CtrlS does it well.

**But Sify's data centre segment alone is already 1.12× the size of all of CtrlS**, which is a useful correction to any sense that CtrlS is the category.

**And the category finding is the one that matters.** Both companies grew EBITDA by more than 23% in their most recent year — CtrlS 23.90%, Sify 30.53% — and **neither converted any of it into profit growth.** CtrlS held PAT exactly flat; Sify's loss nearly doubled. Two independently-reporting Indian data centre operators, neither controlling the other, produced the same conversion outcome in the same window. That is much stronger evidence of a structural cause — debt-funded capex outrunning operating leverage — than either company's numbers alone, and it is the third time in this series that a competitor's disclosure has upgraded a single-company finding into a category one.

## 15. Business Model

CtrlS builds capacity with debt, leases it under medium-to-long-term contracts, and earns an annuity. Revenue is contracted and recurring — ICRA calls it a **"stable annuity revenue model"** with **"healthy client retention"** 🟢 — which is a genuinely good model with one structural property: **the capital is spent years before the annuity arrives, and the interest starts immediately.**

---

## 16. Unit Economics

*Framework note: standard operating-leverage decomposition, run on the four-line cascade. The work is not the margin — the margin is excellent — but noticing where the margin stops.*

| Measure | FY24 | FY25 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating income | ₹1,339 Cr | ₹1,562 Cr | **+16.65%** |
| OPBDITA margin | 46.7% | 49.6% | **+2.9 pp** |
| OPBDITA | ₹625.3 Cr | ₹774.8 Cr | **+23.90%** |
| Implied interest | ~₹118.0 Cr | ~₹198.7 Cr | **+68.38%** |
| Interest coverage | 5.3× | 3.9× | **−26.42%** |
| Total debt / OPBDITA | 3.4× | 3.8× | worse |
| **PAT** | **₹248 Cr** | **₹248 Cr** | **0.00%** |

Every line improves until the fourth, then stops. 🟢

**The cascade is the finding.** Revenue grew 16.65%. Margin expansion turned that into **23.90%** of EBITDA growth — genuine operating leverage, exactly what a capacity business should produce. **And none of it reached profit.** PAT growth as a share of EBITDA growth is **zero**.

**Where it went is derivable from the disclosure.** ICRA publishes interest coverage, so implied interest expense is OPBDITA ÷ coverage: **₹117.98 Cr (FY24) → ₹198.65 Cr (FY25)**, an increase of **₹80.67 Cr**. The OPBDITA increase was **₹149.44 Cr**. So **53.98% of the entire EBITDA increase was consumed by interest**, with the balance absorbed by depreciation on newly commissioned capacity. 🟢

This is not mismanagement and it is not unusual. It is what the first third of a capacity build looks like from inside the P&L, and it is exactly why the FY24–FY25 result is the most useful evidence available about a plan **2.91× larger**.

## 17. Revenue Architecture

Operating income **₹1,339 Cr → ₹1,562 Cr → ₹1,900 Cr (FY26E)**, with ICRA's September 2025 note guiding **20–25% growth in FY26 and FY27**. 🟢 Revenue is contracted annuity from medium-to-long-term leases, so the near-term line is unusually predictable — the uncertainty is not whether the revenue arrives but what it costs to have built the capacity that carries it.

## 18. Cost Architecture

At a ~50% OPBDITA margin, roughly half of revenue covers power, cooling, facility operations and staff. The costs that matter for this analysis sit **below** OPBDITA — depreciation on commissioned capacity and interest on the debt that built it — which is precisely why a margin-led reading of this business is misleading (§16).

---

## 19. The Interest Line

Implied interest rose **68.38% to ~₹198.7 Cr** while revenue rose 16.65%. Coverage fell from **5.3× to 3.9×**, and ICRA projects Total Debt/OPBDITA moving **beyond 4×** over the next 12–18 months. 🟢

The forward figure is the one to hold. ICRA also projects medium-term **DSCR of 1.9–2.3×** with a **downgrade trigger below 1.5×** — so the distance between the plan working and the rating moving is about four-tenths of a turn on a ratio driven by capacity landing on schedule.

## 20. The Capex Plan

**September 2025:** ₹4,500–4,600 Cr across FY26–FY28, funded by ₹3,000–3,500 Cr of debt. **May 2026:** **₹13,000–13,500 Cr across FY27–FY28 alone**, funded 60–65% by debt. 🟢

On midpoints that is **2.91× the capital compressed into two years instead of three — 4.37× the annual run-rate — decided in roughly seven months.** At 62.5% debt funding it implies about **₹8,281 Cr of new borrowing** against an implied ₹2,944 Cr already outstanding, and undrawn facilities of ₹4,665 Cr cover **56.33%** of it.

## 21. Capacity & Build Pipeline

**87 MW (Mar 2024) → 130 MW (Aug 2025) → 150 MW (FY26)**, targeting **950–1,000 MW by FY2029** across 17 operational and 7 under-construction sites. 🟢 The delivered rate over the seventeen months to August 2025 was **43 MW, about 30.35 MW a year**; the guided rate was 40–45 MW; the required rate to hit the midpoint target is **275 MW a year**.

**CtrlS has demonstrated between 11.04% and 15.45% of the build rate its own plan requires**, depending on whether you use delivered or guided capacity. §47 uses the more generous figure.

## 22. Pre-Leasing

ICRA's central rating strength: **"entire incremental large capacities are pre-leased with a hyperscaler,"** with the September 2025 note describing the 40–45 MW annual addition as **"completely pre-leased."** 🟢

This deserves credit before criticism. Pre-leasing eliminates the risk that killed Day 60's thesis and haunts Day 61's — capacity built against hope. CtrlS is doing the disciplined version. The consequence is in §23 and §39: risk removed from one place reappears as leverage in another.

---

## 23. Customer Concentration

**Top 10 customers = ~62% of FY2025 consolidated operating income** 🟢 — and the incremental capacity is pre-leased to a hyperscaler, singular in ICRA's phrasing. So concentration is high today and, on the current plan, structurally increasing: if 825 MW of the next 825 MW is pre-leased to hyperscalers, the top-10 share does not stay at 62%.

---

## 24. Pricing

Colocation is priced per rack or per megawatt under multi-year contracts, with enterprise Tier-4 commanding a premium over wholesale hyperscale capacity. CtrlS does not disclose a price split, which is a material gap (§63) — but the 49.6% blended margin against Sify's 22.0% suggests the enterprise book is currently carrying the economics.

---

## 25. Power & Energy

Power is the largest operating cost and the binding physical constraint on the build: 950–1,000 MW of IT capacity requires grid connections, land and substations that are procured years ahead and are the most common reason data centre schedules slip. ICRA does not disclose CtrlS's PUE, power tariffs or renewable mix (§63), which is why §47's exempt initiative is a power-procurement programme — it is the highest-certainty lever available and it needs no customer to do anything.

---

## 26. Uptime, Tier-4 & SLAs

Tier-4 is CtrlS's founding differentiation: fault-tolerant, concurrently maintainable, the highest uptime classification. It is worth a premium to a bank and close to nothing to a hyperscaler that achieves resilience across availability zones in software rather than buying it in concrete — which means **the company's core technical differentiator is worth least to the customer taking all of its new capacity.**

---

## 27. Sales Motion

Enterprise colocation is a long consultative sale into compliance, security and infrastructure teams, with audits and migration planning measured in quarters. Hyperscale is a capacity negotiation with a procurement function, decided on price per megawatt and delivery date — a completely different motion, and the one that currently determines what gets built.

---

## 28. Technology & Data

CtrlS operates its own facilities, holds Tier-4 certification, and through Schnabel DC Consultants has in-house data centre design capability — a genuine asset when the constraint is building 275 MW a year. What it does not appear to have is a product-level view of which megawatts earn what, by customer type, which is the input §50's proposal requires and §53 tests for.

---

## 29. The Balance Sheet

Implied total debt at FY25 was about **₹2,944 Cr** (3.8× OPBDITA of ₹774.8 Cr). Liquidity is assessed adequate: **₹4,665 Cr of undrawn facilities** at March 2026 and working capital limits of ₹110 Cr at **59% utilisation**. 🟢

The forward arithmetic is what §5 of the thesis rests on: after the FY27–FY28 build, implied debt of roughly **₹11,225 Cr** would require OPBDITA of **₹2,806 Cr — 2.90× the FY26E level** — simply to hold leverage at 4×.

## 30. Org & Leadership

Founder **Sridhar Pinnapureddy** leads the company he started in 2008, with three named wholly-owned subsidiaries covering India operations, Bangalore and design consulting. 🟢

The organisational fact §58 turns on: capacity allocation currently sits wherever the financing conversation happens, because a pre-lease is simultaneously a sales decision and a funding decision. Nobody owns the question of what share of a building should *not* be pre-leased.

## 31. North Star Metric

**EMW/100 — Enterprise Megawatts per 100 MW commissioned.**

The denominator is the design choice, and it is the technique this series has found most reusable. It is **commissioned** megawatts — every megawatt CtrlS brings online, whoever takes it — not megawatts sold. Commission a building that is entirely pre-leased to one hyperscaler and EMW/100 **falls**. The metric makes the trade-off the company is currently making implicitly into a number it has to report.

A megawatt counts toward the numerator only if all four hold, conjunctively:

1. It is **contracted to a non-hyperscaler enterprise** — a bank, insurer, hospital group, government body or mid-market buyer taking racks rather than buildings.
2. It is priced **at or above the published enterprise floor**, so the metric cannot be met by discounting enterprise capacity to hyperscale rates.
3. It is **live and billing for at least two consecutive quarters**, so a signature is not mistaken for revenue.
4. It has **not been reallocated** to a hyperscaler lease during the measurement window.

Condition 2 is what stops the metric being gamed, and condition 4 is what stops the reserve being quietly emptied when a large pre-lease arrives — which is precisely the pressure the proposal exists to resist.

---

## 32. Supporting Metric Tree

EMW/100 decomposes into four inputs different teams can own: **reserved share** (what fraction of each new build is held back), **fill rate** (how much of the reserve gets contracted), **price realisation** against the enterprise floor, and **retention** of reserved megawatts past twelve months.

Reserved share and fill rate must be read together and never separately. A high reserve with a low fill rate is empty concrete earning nothing while interest accrues — the failure mode §40's guardrail is built to catch. A high fill rate with a low reserve means the enterprise demand is real and CtrlS is under-serving it, which is the finding that would justify raising the floor.

---

## 33. Jobs To Be Done

*Framework note: stated for the regulated enterprise buyer, because that is the customer the current allocation is silently deprioritising.*

**"When my regulator requires this workload to sit in India under auditable control, help me get certified capacity on a timeline I can commit to my board — without discovering that the megawatts were sold to a hyperscaler before I finished my security review."**

The current product does this job well when capacity exists and not at all when it does not. A twelve-month enterprise qualification cycle cannot compete for inventory against a pre-lease signed before the foundation is poured, and the enterprise buyer never learns they lost — they are simply told there is no capacity in that city this year.

---

## 34. Kano Analysis

Tier-4 uptime is **must-be** for the regulated enterprise and close to **indifferent** for the hyperscaler, which is the single most important asymmetry in the business. Price per megawatt is **one-dimensional** for hyperscale and secondary for enterprise. Delivery date is **must-be** for both, and is the thing §21 says CtrlS has demonstrated it can hit at 15% of the required rate.

**Capacity availability certainty** — a guarantee that qualified enterprise capacity will exist in a named city on a named date — is currently **absent** rather than indifferent. Nobody offers it, so no buyer has revealed a preference, which argues for testing it cheaply (§54) and against confidence (§47).

---

## 35. Feature Inventory

Tier-4 certified colocation across 8 cities, 17 operational facilities, 150 MW, in-house design capability via Schnabel, and medium-to-long-term annuity contracts with high retention. 🟢

What the inventory does not contain: any commitment about *future* capacity availability to a customer who has not yet finished qualifying. The company sells what exists and pre-sells what does not — but only in building-sized units to counterparties large enough to take one.

---

## 36. Feature Gaps

One gap, stated precisely: **there is no product for a buyer who needs a small amount of certified capacity in eighteen months.** The hyperscaler gets a pre-lease. The enterprise gets whatever is left when it finishes its audit. Nothing in between exists.

The consequence compounds. Each building allocated entirely to a pre-lease is rational on its own terms, and the aggregate of those rational decisions is a company whose Tier-4 differentiation serves a shrinking share of its own capacity.

---

## 37. Switching Costs & Moat

Switching costs for enterprise colocation are genuinely high — migrating a regulated workload is a multi-quarter project with regulatory notification — which is why ICRA cites healthy retention and why the enterprise book carries the margin. For hyperscale, switching costs are low at the portfolio level: capacity is fungible and the next building can go to a competitor.

So CtrlS's moat is concentrated in the customer segment receiving a declining share of new capacity, and absent from the segment receiving nearly all of it.

---

## 38. SWOT

**Strengths.** 49.6% OPBDITA margin, **2.25×** the listed comparator's; [ICRA]AA rating with the rated amount up 48% to ₹3,700 Cr; incremental capacity fully pre-leased; Tier-4 certification and in-house design; ₹4,665 Cr of undrawn facilities; founder-led since 2008.

**Weaknesses.** PAT flat at ₹248 Cr while EBITDA grew 23.90%; interest coverage down 26.42% to 3.9×; top 10 customers 62% of revenue and rising by design; demonstrated build rate 15.45% of required; no disclosed CIN, PUE, power tariff or price split.

**Opportunities.** Regulated-workload demand from DPDP and sectoral localisation; the ~$300 Mn IPO as a deleveraging event; power procurement as a margin lever needing no customer behaviour (§47's winner); enterprise pricing power that Sify's 22% margin suggests is real.

**Threats.** Leverage moving beyond 4× with a 1.5× DSCR downgrade trigger; ICRA's own flag on "stiff competition from large DC addition"; execution risk on 275 MW/year; hyperscaler concentration converting a product company into a contractor.

## 39. Porter's Five Forces — Hyperscale vs Enterprise

*Framework note: run twice in one table, because CtrlS's two customer types invert on nearly every force, and the current allocation is quietly moving the business from the favourable column to the unfavourable one. Seventh consecutive case study where a merged double run said more than a single run would have.*

| Force | Hyperscale (all incremental capacity) | Regulated enterprise (the margin book) |
|---|---|---|
| **Buyer power** | **Very high.** One counterparty takes whole buildings, sets the spec, and has alternatives among "many new players" ICRA names. Pre-lease terms are written by the tenant. | **Low.** Racks not buildings, multi-quarter migration cost, regulator-driven need, and Tier-4 is a genuine requirement rather than a preference. |
| **Supplier power** | **High and physical.** Grid connections, land, substations and long-lead electrical plant gate the 275 MW/year schedule. | **The same constraint**, but a rack-scale buyer can be served from existing headroom. |
| **Rivalry** | **Severe and capitalised.** Every new entrant is chasing the same hyperscale pre-leases with cheaper capital and no legacy margin to protect. | **Limited.** Few Indian operators hold Tier-4 at scale with an audit track record BFSI has already cleared. |
| **New entrants** | **Arriving now** — infrastructure status and global capital have made this the most funded category in Indian tech. | **Slow.** Certification and a regulated-client reference book take years. |
| **Substitutes** | **Strong.** The hyperscaler can self-build, and often does. | **Weak.** Public cloud does not satisfy every localisation and audit requirement. |

Read across, the enterprise column is the better business on four of five forces — and it is the column receiving almost none of the 825 MW being built. **That is the strategic content of §50 in one table.**

## 40. Trust & Safety

**§40 is placed before §50 whenever the proposal creates a harmful incentive. It does here, and the reorder is retained.**

**A published reserve creates an incentive to leave capacity empty.** If EMW/100 is the North Star and a building's reserved megawatts cannot find an enterprise tenant, the honest response is to release them and the metric-serving response is to hold them. Empty commissioned capacity in a debt-funded business is not a neutral outcome — it accrues interest and depreciation while earning nothing, and in the worst case a reserve designed to protect the margin book destroys more value than the concentration it was meant to prevent.

**And a floor can become a pretext for price discrimination.** "Enterprise pricing" applied to a mid-market buyer with no alternative is a premium extracted from captivity rather than earned from Tier-4.

**Guardrail: VUR-90 — Vacant Reserved-capacity Rate at the 90th percentile.** On the decile of buildings with the highest reserved share, the proportion of reserved megawatts still uncontracted **three quarters** after commissioning. Owned by a **Capacity Stewardship function with no sales target**, reporting to the board rather than to commercial. Two consecutive quarters of breach **automatically releases** the reserve on the affected buildings — the release is the default, not a decision someone has to argue for. Enterprise floor pricing is published and reviewed annually against an external benchmark, not set by the sales function.

---

## 41. Accessibility & Inclusion

The enterprise buyers most likely to be squeezed out of capacity are not large banks — they have procurement leverage — but mid-market regulated firms, cooperative banks, regional insurers and hospital groups, who need small amounts of certified capacity and cannot pre-lease a building.

VUR-90 and EMW/100 are therefore reported **by customer size band as well as by building**, so a reserve filled entirely by three large enterprises would not read as success. An aggregate number would hide exactly the buyer the proposal exists to serve.

## 42. Sustainability

Data centres are among the fastest-growing industrial electricity consumers in India, and at 950–1,000 MW CtrlS's own draw becomes a grid-planning question rather than a corporate one. Power procurement, renewable PPAs and PUE improvement are where the environmental and the commercial arguments coincide — which is part of why §47 scores that programme highest.

**CtrlS's PUE, tariff structure and renewable mix are not disclosed in either rationale** (§63), so nothing quantitative is claimed here.

CtrlS is positioned as India's Tier-4 operator — the trusted, certified, enterprise-grade choice. The current allocation of capacity points the company at a customer for whom that positioning is nearly irrelevant.

A company can hold a premium position and sell wholesale capacity, but not indefinitely and not silently. §50 is, in part, an argument that the positioning is worth defending with a published constraint rather than an intention.

## 48. Incentive Design

Three exclusions, permanent, because each converts the proposal into the harm §40 describes.

**No sales commission on reserved capacity.** Paying on reserve fills creates pressure to book weak enterprise tenants at the floor price, which fails North Star condition 2 and produces churn a year later.

**No commercial authority to release the reserve.** Release is automatic on VUR-90 breach and otherwise requires Capacity Stewardship sign-off. A reserve that the sales function can waive when a large pre-lease appears is not a reserve; it is a preference.

**No enterprise floor price set by the team that sells against it.** Published, benchmarked externally, reviewed annually.

## 49. What I Would Not Build

**Not a refusal to serve hyperscalers.** They are creditworthy, they pre-lease, and they are why the financing exists. The proposal reserves a share, not a veto.

**Not a REIT or asset-monetisation vehicle.** It would address the leverage arithmetic in §29 and would say nothing about what the company sells or to whom — a financing answer to a product question.

**Not a public-cloud or managed-services pivot.** Sify's numbers show the adjacent digital services business shrinking 2% while data centres grew 23%; the demand is in capacity, not in moving up the stack.

**Not a price increase on the existing enterprise book.** §40's second harm exactly: extracting from captivity rather than earning from Tier-4.

## 50. Proposal — CtrlS Regulated Capacity

**A published, lender-visible floor reserving a defined share of every new build for regulated Indian workloads at enterprise pricing.**

Three parts. A **reserved share** — a stated percentage of commissioned megawatts in each new building, held back from pre-lease and available to non-hyperscaler enterprise buyers for a defined window. A **published enterprise floor price**, benchmarked externally and reviewed annually, so the reserve cannot be filled by discounting. And **disclosure**: the reserved share, the fill rate and the vacancy are reported to lenders and, post-IPO, to investors — which is what converts an intention into a constraint.

The case rests on §39 and §23 together. §39 shows the enterprise column is the better business on four of five forces — lower buyer power, weaker rivalry, slower entrants, weaker substitutes — and carries the margin that makes CtrlS's 49.6% possible against Sify's 22.0%. §23 shows that segment is receiving almost none of the 825 MW being built, because a pre-lease signed before construction always beats a twelve-month enterprise qualification for the same inventory.

The instrument is deliberately a **self-imposed, externally-visible constraint** rather than a product feature, because the failure it addresses is not a missing capability — CtrlS can already sell to enterprises — but a sequencing advantage that hyperscale demand holds structurally. Constraints that live only inside the company get waived the first time a large pre-lease arrives; that is why §48 removes commercial authority over release and §40 makes release automatic on breach.

It is deliberately narrow: new builds only, a single-digit-to-low-double-digit reserved share, three cities, and killable in two analyst-weeks (§53).

---

## 51. Proposal Mechanics

**The reserved share.** A stated percentage of each new building's commissioned megawatts, set per city on evidence of regulated demand rather than uniformly. The pilot tests **10%**.

**The window.** Reserved capacity is held for a defined period after commissioning — the pilot uses **three quarters**, matched to VUR-90 — after which unfilled megawatts release automatically to general inventory including hyperscale.

**The floor price.** Published, benchmarked against external enterprise colocation pricing, reviewed annually, and outside the sales function's control (§48). A megawatt let below the floor does not count toward EMW/100.

**Disclosure.** Reserved share, fill rate, realised price against floor, and VUR-90 reported to lenders quarterly and in the offer document if the IPO proceeds. **The disclosure is the mechanism** — a reserve nobody external can see is a policy, and policies get waived.

**Governance.** Capacity Stewardship (§58) sets the share and the floor and owns release. Commercial sells against both and controls neither.

**What is not built.** No hyperscaler exclusion, no REIT, no managed-services pivot, no repricing of the existing book (§49).

---

## 52. Proposal Economics

The mechanism is not that the reserve earns more per megawatt in year one — it may well earn less, because a filling reserve is by definition slower to contract than a pre-lease. It is that it **changes what the company owns at the end of the build**: either a 950 MW portfolio concentrated in counterparties with alternatives, or the same portfolio with a defended enterprise book that carries the margin and the switching costs.

The cost is quantifiable in principle and unquantifiable here: reserved megawatts that fill slowly carry interest and depreciation while earning nothing, which is precisely the drag §16 identified. That is why the guardrail is a vacancy metric rather than a revenue one, and why release is automatic.

**This case study does not model the size of the effect and will not**, because the enterprise-versus-hyperscale price split is not disclosed (§24, §63) and it is the first input required. The mechanism is clear, the magnitude is unknown, and §53 buys the magnitude for two analyst-weeks. If enterprise pricing does not carry a real premium, the whole proposal collapses — which is K1.

---

## 53. Phase 0 — Two Analyst-Weeks, With Kill Criteria

Phase 0 builds nothing and uses contracts CtrlS already holds: the last three years of enterprise and hyperscale leases, by city, with realised price per megawatt.

**K1 — the enterprise premium is not real.** If realised enterprise price per megawatt does not exceed hyperscale by at least **25%** after adjusting for contract length and service scope, there is no margin to defend, the 49.6% blended margin is coming from somewhere else, and the proposal has no economic basis.

**K2 — named as the criterion most likely to fire.** If enterprise demand in the three pilot cities cannot absorb a 10% reserve within three quarters on historical enquiry-to-contract rates, the reserve is empty concrete and the proposal's cost exceeds its benefit. **The demonstrated enterprise sales cycle is long and the reserve window is short — bet on this one.**

**K3 — the data does not exist.** If lease records cannot be segmented into enterprise versus hyperscale with realised pricing to ±10%, EMW/100 cannot be computed and nothing here is measurable. §28 suggests this is a live possibility.

Any of the three firing stops the work.

---

## 54. Experiment Design & Falsification

**Arm A — control.** Current allocation: capacity goes to whoever contracts first.

**Arm B — the cheap imitation, and the real test.** An **internal, undisclosed reservation target** — the same 10% held back, tracked internally, with no publication to lenders and no automatic release. This is a memo, deliverable immediately, and it supplies the *allocation* without the *disclosure* that is the proposal's entire justification. **If Arm B holds the reserve as well as Arm C, the disclosure apparatus is theatre — ship the memo and stop.**

**Arm C — full Regulated Capacity:** published share, published floor, external disclosure, automatic release, Capacity Stewardship governance.

**Pre-registered decision rule R1.** Arm C proceeds beyond pilot only if it beats Arm B by more than **6 percentage points on EMW/100 across four quarters spanning at least one large hyperscale pre-lease negotiation**, with **VUR-90 no worse than baseline in every pilot city measured separately** (§41).

The condition about spanning a pre-lease negotiation is the whole test. A reserve is easy to hold when nobody wants the capacity; the only informative period is the one where a large tenant is asking for the building.

**What would falsify the thesis rather than the proposal.** §16 claims the growth is being consumed by the cost of financing it. That is falsified if FY26's PAT grows materially on FY25's ₹248 Cr while leverage rises — no experiment needed, ICRA will publish it.

## 56. Roadmap

Phase 0 is two analyst-weeks on existing lease data (§53). If it survives, a pilot reserving 10% in three cities across four quarters including one live hyperscale negotiation. If R1 clears, extension to all new builds with the share set per city.

Portfolio-wide publication is deliberately **not** on this roadmap until VUR-90 has held through a full pilot, because a published reserve that visibly sits empty is worse for a company approaching an IPO than no published reserve at all.

---

## 57. Go-To-Market

The reserve is sold through the existing enterprise motion — compliance, security and infrastructure buyers, with audit support — but with one thing that motion has never had: **a date**. "Certified capacity will exist in this city in this quarter" is the artefact §33 says is missing, and it is only credible because the reserve is published.

For lenders and, later, investors, the framing is risk reduction rather than growth: a disclosed enterprise floor is a concentration-mitigation instrument, which is exactly what a rating agency flagging client concentration wants to see.

---

## 58. Org Changes Required

One new function, before the first published reserve rather than after. **Capacity Stewardship** owns the reserved share, the enterprise floor price, VUR-90 and release authority; carries no sales target; reports to the board.

The structural problem from §30 is that capacity allocation is currently a by-product of the financing conversation, because a pre-lease is simultaneously a sale and a funding event. Nobody owns the question of what should be held back, which is why the answer has been "nothing."

---

## 59. Success Criteria

At twelve months from pilot: EMW/100 rising across the pilot cities; reserve fill rate above 60% within the three-quarter window; realised enterprise price at or above the published floor; VUR-90 no worse than baseline in every city separately.

The criterion that actually matters is the fifth: **the reserve survived a large hyperscale pre-lease negotiation without being waived.** If it did not, the instrument does not work and the honest conclusion is that CtrlS is a build-to-suit business that should be valued and managed as one.

---

## 60. What Would Change My Mind

If FY26 PAT grows materially on two flat years, the financing drag is temporary and §16's thesis fails.

If K1 fires and enterprise pricing carries no real premium over hyperscale, there is no margin book to defend and the 49.6% margin is explained by something else entirely — in which case the proposal is solving an imagined problem.

If commissioned capacity in FY27 lands near 275 MW, the execution concern in §21 is wrong, the stress rule in §47 is far too harsh, and the proposal deserves a much better hearing than this file gives it.

If the top-10 concentration **falls** while the build accelerates, then hyperscale demand is more distributed than ICRA's singular phrasing implies, and §23's premise weakens considerably.

And if a DRHP appears with a disclosed enterprise-versus-hyperscale revenue split, most of what is assumed here becomes checkable — which is the outcome this case study would most like.

## 62. Limitations

**This analysis works entirely from rating rationales, not from company filings.** CtrlS is private, has no DRHP on file, and does not publish results. ICRA's rationales are rigorous and independent, but they are summaries prepared for a credit purpose, and they disclose what a lender needs rather than what a product analyst wants.

Consequently: **no CIN, no PUE, no power tariff, no enterprise-versus-hyperscale price split, no unit volumes, no segment P&L.** Interest expense in §16 and §19 is *derived* from disclosed coverage ratios, not disclosed directly.

Several figures are **midpoints of disclosed ranges** — capex, capacity target, build rate, FY26E margin — and every ratio built on them inherits that. Where a range would change a conclusion it is given as a range (§21, §47).

No primary research was conducted; personas in §11 are constructed from ICRA's client-mix description and labelled as such in ASSUMPTIONS Part 3.
