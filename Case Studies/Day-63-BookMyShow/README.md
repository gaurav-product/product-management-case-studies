# BookMyShow — Growing Into The Cheaper Half Of Its Own Industry

> BookMyShow's FY25 reads as a successful pivot: total income up 30.70% to ₹1,869 Cr, profit up **76.15% to ₹192 Cr**, live events revenue up **66.15% to ₹756 Cr**, and KKR signing for a 6% stake on 19 August 2026. Of the ₹439 Cr of income growth, **live events supplied ₹301 Cr (68.56%) and movie ticketing ₹87 Cr (19.82%)** — extend each segment's own growth rate one year and live events overtakes ticketing. That is the pivot working. It is also the problem. **Live Nation is the only listed company running both halves of this industry at scale, and its FY2025 segments read: concerts 82.94% of revenue at a 3.29% margin; ticketing 12.30% of revenue at a 35.48% margin.** Ticketing earns **3.73× its revenue share in profit**; concerts earn **0.35×**. BookMyShow is shifting its mix out of the 35% business and into the 3% one. And the headline understates how much: profit before tax grew **50.00%**, not 76.15% — a **₹27 Cr swing in the tax line, 14.06% of profit**, supplied the difference.

## 1. Executive Summary

BookMyShow is India's dominant entertainment ticketing platform, operating since 2007 across **more than 700 towns and cities**. 🟢 On **19 August 2026** KKR signed a definitive agreement for a **6% minority stake**, reported at **$40–50 Mn**, explicitly to scale the live entertainment business. 🟡

FY25 (year ended March 2025) was a strong year on every headline. Total income **₹1,430 Cr → ₹1,869 Cr (+30.70%)**; total expenses **₹1,320 Cr → ₹1,704 Cr (+29.09%)**, growing 1.61 points slower than income; profit after tax **₹109 Cr → ₹192 Cr (+76.15%)**. 🟢 Real operating leverage in a business that spent years unprofitable.

Three disclosed facts complicate the reading.

**The mix is shifting fast, and it is shifting toward the cheaper business.** Online ticketing grew **11.74%** to ₹828 Cr; live events grew **66.15%** to ₹756 Cr — **5.63× the ticketing growth rate**. Live events supplied **68.56%** of income growth against ticketing's **19.82%**. Ticketing fell from **51.82% to 44.30%** of income while live rose from **31.82% to 40.45%**. The FY25 gap between them is **₹72 Cr**; extend each segment's own rate one year and live events passes ticketing. 🟢

**And the industry's own economics say that is the wrong direction.** Live Nation FY2025: **concerts $20.9 Bn revenue, $687 Mn adjusted operating income — a 3.29% margin. Ticketing $3.1 Bn revenue, $1.1 Bn AOI — 35.48%.** Ticketing is 12.30% of revenue and **45.83% of profit**; concerts are 82.94% of revenue and 28.63% of profit. The ticketing margin is **10.80× the concerts margin**, at the company that has been running both for two decades at 128× BookMyShow's scale. 🟢

**The profit headline is also softer than it looks.** Income less expenses implies profit before tax of **₹110 Cr (FY24) → ₹165 Cr (FY25), growth of 50.00%** — against reported PAT growth of 76.15%. The gap is the tax line: a ₹1 Cr charge in FY24 became a **₹27 Cr credit** in FY25, **14.06% of reported profit**. 🟡 This is derived, not disclosed (§19, Appendix A-2).

None of this makes the pivot wrong. Live events are where the demand growth is, the category is genuinely booming, and KKR is not buying 6% of a bad idea. The question this case study asks is narrower and, I think, more useful: **BookMyShow is taking on promoter risk — committing money to artists and venues before knowing demand — while holding the one asset that could price that risk better than anyone in India, and it does not yet appear to use it as a gate.**

The proposal is the **Demand Gate**: a published, pre-commitment demand threshold every company-risk event must clear before BookMyShow puts its own balance sheet behind it, with the gate's forecast accuracy tracked and reported. North Star **GRE/100 — Gated Risk Events per 100 company-risk events**. Guardrail **NAS-90 — New-Artist Share at the 90th percentile of gate-rejection intensity**, because a gate that only approves safe, known artists kills the discovery that makes the slate worth having.

Under a stress test built from BookMyShow's own numbers — ticketing's **19.82%** share of income growth — the Demand Gate falls from 3rd of 4 initiatives to **last**.

## 5. Product Overview

BookMyShow sells tickets — to films, and increasingly to concerts, comedy, sport and festivals — across **700+ towns and cities**, and through BookMyShow Live it also promotes and produces events rather than merely selling seats for them. 🟢 That second activity is a different business wearing the same brand: a marketplace takes a fee on someone else's risk, a promoter takes the risk.

---

## 6. History & Timeline

Big Tree Entertainment was incorporated in **1999**; the BookMyShow product launched in **2007** and spent the following decade becoming the default way Indians buy cinema tickets. 🟢 The recent chapter is the live pivot: live events revenue **₹455 Cr (FY24) → ₹756 Cr (FY25)**, and a KKR investment in August 2026 explicitly to accelerate it.

---

## 7. Market Context

Indian live entertainment is in a genuine demand boom — international tours now routing through India, festivals scaling, and discretionary spend on experiences rising faster than on goods. BookMyShow sits at the centre of it with the only national ticketing rail, which is why a global investor bought in.

The structural caution comes from the mature version of this market: at Live Nation, two decades in, the concert business still earns a **3.29%** operating margin (§14). Booming demand and thin margins are not contradictory — they are the normal condition of live promotion.

---

## 8. Regulatory Context

Three regulatory surfaces matter and none is currently binding hard.

**Convenience fees have a litigation history in India.** Courts and consumer forums have periodically challenged online booking fees, and cinema-ticket pricing is a state subject where caps have been imposed before. Since the ticketing business is where the margin is (§14), any fee intervention lands directly on the profitable half.

**Live events carry a permissions burden that scales with ambition** — venue licensing, police and fire clearances, sound and curfew limits, and local political sensitivity that can cancel an event late. Cancellation risk sits with whoever holds the promoter contract, which is increasingly BookMyShow.

**Consumer-protection and refund rules** apply asymmetrically: for a film the platform is an intermediary; for an event it has promoted, it is closer to the principal. That distinction is invisible on the app and material to liability, and §40 returns to it.

---

## 9. Problem Statement

**For the business:** BookMyShow is converting a fee-taking marketplace into a risk-taking promoter, at speed, and the industry's own best evidence says the destination earns roughly a tenth of the margin of the origin. Growth is real; the mix is moving toward the part of the industry that historically does not pay.

**For a product organisation:** promotion is a series of large, irreversible bets — artist fees, venue holds, marketing commitments — made months before anyone knows whether the show sells. BookMyShow holds India's best pre-purchase demand signal and, on the evidence available, does not appear to have turned it into a formal gate on those bets.

---

## 10. Who Actually Pays

FY25 total income of ₹1,869 Cr splits into **online ticketing ₹828 Cr (44.30%)**, **live events ₹756 Cr (40.45%)**, and a residual of **₹285 Cr (15.25%)** covering everything else. 🟢

The residual is not trivial and is not broken out — it supplied **11.62%** of the year's income growth. What sits in it (sponsorship, food and beverage, advertising, international) matters to any margin reading and is named in §63 as a gap.

---

## 11. User Personas

*Constructed from disclosed reach and product surface, not primary research — see ASSUMPTIONS Part 3.*

**The film-goer** books two to six times a year, decides in the last 48 hours, cares about seat, showtime and price, and is the base BookMyShow was built on. **The live attendee** buys rarely but at a much higher ticket price, decides months ahead, and treats the purchase as an event in itself — the queue, the drop, the sold-out notification. **The artist or promoter** is the customer nobody sees: they choose a partner on reach, settlement terms and, increasingly, on who will underwrite the risk.

---

## 12. User Journey

Discover, choose, pay, receive a QR code, attend. For film that journey is short and habitual; for live it starts months earlier with an announcement, often a pre-sale window, and a decision made under scarcity.

The gap worth noticing: between the announcement and the on-sale, **thousands of people signal intent** — notification sign-ups, reminders, waitlist joins, repeated visits — and that signal arrives *before* the promoter's financial commitment is fully locked. §50 is built entirely in that window.

## 13. TAM / SAM / SOM — Restricted Form

*Framework note: restricted form, sized from the company's own disclosed base. No primary-sourced Indian live-entertainment market size could be verified to this series' standard (§63); default since Day 57.*

The disclosed base is **₹1,869 Cr of FY25 total income** across **700+ towns and cities**, of which **₹756 Cr** is live events.

The honest way to size the opportunity is by margin structure rather than market size. If BookMyShow's live business eventually resembles Live Nation's concerts segment at **3.29%** operating margin, then ₹756 Cr of live revenue carries roughly ₹25 Cr of operating profit; if it resembles the ticketing segment at **35.48%**, roughly ₹268 Cr. **The entire strategic question is which of those two numbers the live business converges toward**, and BookMyShow does not disclose segment profitability (§63). That is Assumption A1 and §53 measures it first.

## 14. Competitive Landscape — Restricted To Filers

*Framework note: restricted to competitors whose numbers are filed. **Live Nation Entertainment is NYSE-listed and reports Concerts and Ticketing as separate segments** — the rarest and most useful thing available here. **PVR INOX files and District (Zomato/Eternal's live-events arm) sits inside a listed parent; both are named rather than ignored, neither is deep-dived.** BookMyShow's own figures are as reported from MCA filings, not from the company.*

**Live Nation, FY2025: revenue $25.2 Bn (+9%), adjusted operating income $2.4 Bn**, a group AOI margin of **9.52%**. The segments are the finding:

| Segment | Revenue | Share of revenue | AOI | Share of AOI | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Concerts** | $20.9 Bn | **82.94%** | $687 Mn | **28.63%** | **3.29%** |
| **Ticketing** | $3.1 Bn | **12.30%** | $1.1 Bn | **45.83%** | **35.48%** |

🟢 Three readings.

**The margin gap is an order of magnitude.** Ticketing's margin is **10.80×** concerts'. This is not a bad year or a one-off; it is the structure of the industry at its most mature operator.

**Profit and revenue live in different segments.** Ticketing earns **3.73×** its revenue share in profit; concerts earn **0.35×**. Live Nation is, in profit terms, a ticketing company that also runs the world's largest concert business — and the concerts exist substantially to feed the ticketing.

**Which makes BookMyShow's mix shift a direction worth questioning.** BookMyShow is moving from 51.82% ticketing toward a live-majority mix. Live Nation, at **128× the revenue**, has never made concerts pay like ticketing and does not appear to be trying to. The right reading is not that BookMyShow should stop — the demand is real and the option value of owning the live rail is genuine — but that **the live business should be run as a customer-acquisition and inventory-control engine for ticketing, not as a profit centre in its own right**, and measured accordingly.

## 15. Business Model

Two models under one brand. **Ticketing** is a marketplace: BookMyShow lists someone else's inventory, takes a booking fee, carries no inventory risk, and scales with almost no marginal cost. **Live events** is promotion: BookMyShow commits to artist fees, venue holds and marketing before a ticket is sold, and keeps what is left.

The first is a software business. The second is a working-capital business with a hit rate. They are reported as one company and, on the evidence available, measured as one company.

## 16. Unit Economics

*Framework note: the standard mix-shift decomposition. The work is not the margin — BookMyShow does not disclose one by segment — but establishing which segment supplied the growth, and what the industry says that segment is worth.*

| Measure | FY24 | FY25 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total income | ₹1,430 Cr | ₹1,869 Cr | **+30.70%** |
| Online ticketing | ₹741 Cr | ₹828 Cr | **+11.74%** |
| Live events | ₹455 Cr | ₹756 Cr | **+66.15%** |
| Total expenses | ₹1,320 Cr | ₹1,704 Cr | +29.09% |
| Implied PBT | ₹110 Cr | ₹165 Cr | **+50.00%** |
| **Reported PAT** | **₹109 Cr** | **₹192 Cr** | **+76.15%** |

🟢 (PBT is derived — §19, Appendix A-2.)

**The growth decomposition is the finding.** Of ₹439 Cr of income growth: **live events ₹301 Cr (68.56%)**, **ticketing ₹87 Cr (19.82%)**, residual ₹51 Cr (11.62%). Live events grew at **5.63×** the ticketing rate and contributed **3.46×** as many rupees.

**The mix moved 7.5 points in one year.** Ticketing **51.82% → 44.30%** of income; live **31.82% → 40.45%**. The remaining gap between them is **₹72 Cr**. Extend each segment's own FY25 growth rate a single year and ticketing reaches ~₹925 Cr while live reaches ~₹1,256 Cr — **live overtakes, and not narrowly.**

**Operating leverage is real.** Expenses grew 29.09% against income at 30.70%, a 1.61-point gap, lifting PBT margin from **7.69% to 8.83%**. This is a company that has learned to grow without spending proportionally, and that deserves saying plainly before §19 qualifies the headline.

## 17. Revenue Architecture

Income is ticketing fees, live event revenue, and an undisclosed residual of **₹285 Cr (15.25% of FY25 income)** that supplied 11.62% of the year's growth. 🟢 The residual most plausibly holds sponsorship, advertising, food and beverage and international operations, but it is not broken out and nothing here rests on its composition (§63).

## 18. Cost Architecture

Total expenses **₹1,704 Cr, up 29.09%**, against income up 30.70%. 🟢 BookMyShow does not disclose a cost breakdown, which matters more than usual here: **promoter costs — artist fees, venue hire, production — behave completely differently from marketplace costs**, being largely committed before revenue and largely unrecoverable if a show underperforms. A single blended expense line cannot show that, and the absence is the reason §50 exists.

---

## 19. The Tax Line

Income less expenses implies profit before tax of **₹110 Cr (FY24)** and **₹165 Cr (FY25)** — growth of **50.00%**. Reported PAT grew **76.15%**. The difference is **26.15 percentage points**, and it sits in tax: an implied charge of **₹1 Cr** in FY24 (a 0.91% effective rate) became an implied **credit of ₹27 Cr** in FY25, or **14.06% of reported profit**. 🟡

Two honest caveats. This is **derived from two reported aggregates, not from a disclosed tax line** (Appendix A-2), and "total income" as reported may not be the exact base a statutory PBT would use. And a deferred-tax credit is a perfectly normal event for a company that has just turned durably profitable and can now recognise accumulated losses as an asset — it is not aggressive accounting.

But it does mean the number in the headlines is not the number the business earned. **PBT growth of 50.00% is the operating result; 76.15% is the operating result plus a one-time recognition.** Anyone underwriting the FY26 comparison should use the former.

## 20. The Two Businesses

Ticketing: no inventory risk, near-zero marginal cost, revenue recognised on a fee, and — per §14 — the segment that carries the industry's profit. Live promotion: capital committed before demand is known, costs largely fixed once contracted, and per §14 a **3.29%** margin at the most mature operator in the world.

The strategic error available here is not doing live events. It is running the two on one P&L, one set of incentives and one measurement frame, so that live-event revenue growth reads as company progress regardless of what it earns.

## 21. Live Events

**₹455 Cr → ₹756 Cr, +66.15%**, and the explicit purpose of the KKR investment. 🟢 It is the fastest-growing thing BookMyShow has and the reason a global sponsor bought in.

It is also the half where a single mis-priced stadium show can absorb a meaningful share of a year's profit — ₹192 Cr of PAT is not many failed arena tours — and BookMyShow discloses nothing about slate composition, hit rate or per-event economics (§63).

---

## 22. Movie Ticketing

**₹741 Cr → ₹828 Cr, +11.74%.** 🟢 Slow, and easy to read as the mature, boring half. Per §14 it is also, in all probability, where the profit is: Live Nation's ticketing segment earns 45.83% of group profit on 12.30% of revenue.

An 11.74% growth rate on the profitable segment is not a problem to be fixed by growing something else faster. It is the asset that funds everything, and §47 scores optimising it as the highest-value initiative available.

---

## 23. Demand & Seasonality

Film demand is continuous with release-driven spikes; live demand is lumpy, announcement-driven, and concentrated in a few tours and festivals a year. The combination is volatile at the quarter level and BookMyShow reports annually, so nothing in the public record shows the volatility.

---

## 24. Pricing & Convenience Fees

Ticketing monetises through a booking or convenience fee — a percentage or flat charge on someone else's ticket price. Live events monetise the ticket itself, so BookMyShow's revenue per live ticket is far larger and its *margin* per live ticket is very likely far smaller (§14).

This is why revenue-mix charts flatter the pivot: **a rupee of live revenue and a rupee of ticketing revenue are not the same rupee**, and no public disclosure lets an outsider convert between them.

---

## 25. Supply — Venues, Cinemas, Artists

Ticketing supply is cinema chains — concentrated, contractual, renewed periodically. Live supply is artists, their agents, and venues — relationship-driven, auction-like for the biggest tours, and increasingly contested as new money enters Indian live entertainment.

The asymmetry that matters: **cinema supply renews, artist supply is re-won every tour.** A promoter's negotiating position rests on reach and on willingness to guarantee, and guarantees are exactly the risk §50 is about.

---

## 26. Risk In Promotion

A promoter commits artist fees, venue holds, production and marketing months ahead, against demand that is unknown until the on-sale. If the show sells, margin is thin but positive; if it does not, the loss is close to the full commitment.

This is the structural reason concerts earn 3.29% at Live Nation while ticketing earns 35.48%: **the promoter is being paid to absorb variance.** BookMyShow has begun absorbing that variance and holds an unusually good instrument for pricing it (§28) — which is the entire argument of this case study.

---

## 27. Distribution & Reach

**More than 700 towns and cities**, a national app installed base built over eighteen years, and the default position in Indian cinema ticketing. 🟢 This is the genuine moat and it is what KKR bought into: no competitor can assemble equivalent demand-side reach quickly, whatever they pay artists.

---

## 28. Technology & Data

The asset that matters for §50: **BookMyShow observes intent before it observes purchase.** Notification sign-ups, reminder-setting, waitlist joins, repeat product views and pre-sale registrations all accumulate in the window between an event announcement and its on-sale — and that window sits before, or alongside, the promoter's financial commitment.

No competitor in India holds comparable pre-purchase signal at national scale. Whether BookMyShow uses it as a formal input to promoter decisions is not disclosed; §53's Phase 0 is designed to find out cheaply from data the company already has.

**Definitive agreement 19 August 2026: KKR acquiring a 6% minority stake**, reported at **$40–50 Mn**, subject to regulatory approvals, advised by Avendus Capital and Trilegal, alongside existing holders Network18, Accel, Elevation Capital, Stripes Group and TPG. 🟡

At 6%, the reported range implies a valuation of **$666.7–833.3 Mn**, roughly **₹6,333–7,917 Cr** at ₹95/USD — **33.0× to 41.2× FY25 PAT**, or **3.39× to 4.24× FY25 income**. The **25% spread between the two reported amounts** is itself unresolved and is treated as a range throughout (Appendix A-3). ## 30. Org & Leadership

BookMyShow is founder-led and has been through several ownership configurations, with Network18 a longstanding shareholder alongside Accel, Elevation, Stripes and TPG, and now KKR. 🟡

The organisational fact §58 turns on: **the decision to commit money to an event and the data that could price that decision sit in different functions.** Live events is a commercial and relationships business; the demand signal lives in product and data. Nobody obviously owns the join.

## 31. North Star Metric

**GRE/100 — Gated Risk Events per 100 company-risk events.**

The denominator is the choice that matters. It is not *all* events, and not events sold: it is **events on which BookMyShow puts its own money at risk** (§26). Anything the company merely ticketed for a third-party promoter is excluded, because no variance was absorbed.

The definition is **conjunctive**. An event counts in the numerator only if all four hold:

1. It passed a **published demand gate before financial commitment** — not after.
2. Realised attendance landed **within ±20%** of the gate's forecast.
3. Contribution after artist, venue, production and marketing cost was **positive**.
4. **No post-hoc override** was recorded on the decision.

Each clause closes a specific way the metric could be gamed: gating after the money is spent, a forecast so wide it cannot be wrong, an event that fills but loses money, and an exception process that quietly becomes the norm.

The denominator does the hardest work. **Taking on more risk without gating it lowers the metric mechanically.** A team that wants a higher GRE/100 can raise it only by gating more or by risking less — both of which are the intended behaviour. It cannot be raised by growing.

---

## 32. Supporting Metric Tree

**GRE/100** decomposes into: *gate coverage* (share of company-risk events that entered the gate at all) × *gate pass rate* × *forecast accuracy within ±20%* × *contribution-positive rate* × *(1 − override rate)*.

Each factor is separately observable and separately fixable. A falling GRE/100 tells you which of the five moved, which is the property §55's early-warning indicators depend on.

---

## 33. Jobs To Be Done

**Audience:** *when an artist I care about announces a date, help me not miss it and not overpay.* **Cinema-goer:** *get me a seat for tonight without a phone call.* **Promoter or artist:** *fill the room and get paid.* **Cinema chain:** *fill seats I have already paid to run.*

The pivot to live changes which job BookMyShow is being paid for: from the fourth to the third. The third pays more per event and pays worse per rupee (§24).

---

## 34. Kano Analysis

**Basic:** seat selection, payment, ticket delivery, refunds. Failure here is fatal; excellence is invisible. **Performance:** breadth of what is listed, price, speed of on-sale under load. **Delighter:** genuine pre-sale access, accurate reminders for events a user would otherwise miss.

The delighters are exactly the surfaces that generate the intent signal §28 describes — which is the argument for treating them as instrumentation, not only as features.

---

## 35. Feature Inventory

Discovery and listings across films, live events, sports and activities; seat-level selection; payments; a national app and web presence; notifications, reminders and waitlists; pre-sale registration; ticketing infrastructure for venues; and, increasingly, the promoter-side functions of a live business — booking, production and on-ground operations. 🟡

## 36. Feature Gaps

Nothing in the public record describes a **formal, published decision gate between the demand signal the platform generates and the financial commitment the live business makes** (§28, §30). Whether one exists internally is not disclosed 🔴 — §53 is designed to establish that cheaply before anything is built.

## 37. Switching Costs & Moat

For a consumer, switching cost is near zero for any single transaction — and near total at the moment of scarcity, because the ticket exists on exactly one platform. The moat is not habit; it is **exclusive supply plus reach in 700+ towns** (§27).

That moat is strongest in ticketing, where cinema relationships renew, and weakest in live, where artist supply is re-won every tour (§25). **The pivot moves the business away from where its moat is deepest.**

---

## 38. SWOT

**Strengths.** Default position in Indian cinema ticketing; **700+ towns** of reach 🟢; eighteen years of demand data (§28); newly profitable — **PAT ₹192 Cr, +76.15%** 🟢; a shareholder register that now includes KKR (§29).

**Weaknesses.** Growth is concentrating in the **structurally low-margin half** — Live Nation's concerts segment earns **3.29%** against ticketing's **35.48%**, a **10.80×** gap 🟢; **₹285 Cr (15.25%)** of FY25 income is not broken out at all 🔴; the profit headline depends on a **₹27 Cr tax credit worth 14.06% of PAT** 🟡; annual-only reporting hides quarter-level volatility in a lumpy business (§23).

**Opportunities.** Indian live entertainment is early and expanding; the intent signal in §28 is unmatched at national scale; KKR capital is explicitly earmarked for live (§29).

**Threats.** Well-funded entrants bidding for the same artists; a single failed marquee tour is a material loss against **₹192 Cr** of profit; regulatory attention to convenience fees (§8); and the possibility that the fee-earning half funds the risk-bearing half indefinitely.

## 39. Porter's Five Forces — Ticketing vs Live, Run Twice

The business has two halves with opposed structures, so the framework is run twice and merged.

| Force | **Ticketing** (fee on someone else's risk) | **Live events** (own the risk) |
|---|---|---|
| **Supplier power** | Moderate — cinema chains are concentrated but need distribution as much as BookMyShow needs inventory; contracts renew | **High** — artists and their agents are scarce, re-won every tour, and increasingly courted by funded rivals (§25) |
| **Buyer power** | Low at the moment of scarcity; the seat exists on one platform | Low per transaction, but demand is unknown until on-sale — buyers exercise power by not showing up |
| **New entrants** | **Low** — 700+ towns of reach cannot be assembled quickly (§27) | **High** — promotion needs capital and relationships, not distribution; money entering the category buys in directly |
| **Substitutes** | Box office, streaming, piracy | Streaming, sport, festivals, any competing use of a weekend |
| **Rivalry** | Moderate — a settled category | **High and rising** — bidding for the same finite artist supply |

The pattern is unambiguous: **on four of five forces, live is the worse structure.** The half BookMyShow is growing fastest (§21) is the half where it holds least power — which is what Live Nation's **10.80×** margin gap is measuring (§14).

## 47. Prioritisation — RICE With A Stress Test

Reach is **₹ Cr of FY25 total income touched**; effort is in person-quarters.

**The stress rule comes from the company's own disclosure.** Ticketing supplied **19.82%** of FY25 income growth (§17). Any initiative whose return depends on the *growth* continuing to arrive where it arrived this year is multiplied by that share. Venue and inventory supply lock-ins are exempt: they pay off from the installed base, not from next year's growth mix.

The honest reading is that the stress test says something blunt: *the cheapest, highest-confidence thing BookMyShow could do is get better at the half it is de-emphasising.*

**I am proposing the Demand Gate anyway**, and §49 says what would have to be true for that to be wrong.

## 48. Incentive Design

## 49. What I Would Not Build

**Not a recommendation engine for which events to stage.** The gate's value is that it is a *published threshold with a recorded forecast*, not a black box — a model that quietly scores events reproduces the accountability gap in §30 with better mathematics.

**Not a retreat from live.** §47's stress test favours ticketing, but a fee business with no owned inventory has no answer to §37's observation that its moat is deepest exactly where growth is slowest.

**Not exclusivity-by-guarantee.** Winning artists by out-guaranteeing rivals is the mechanism that produced Live Nation's **3.29%** (§14).

## 50. Proposal — The Demand Gate

**A published, pre-commitment demand threshold that every company-risk event must clear before BookMyShow puts its own balance sheet behind it — with the gate's forecast accuracy tracked and reported afterwards.**

The problem it addresses is stated in §26: a promoter commits artist fees, venue holds, production and marketing months before demand is observable, and if the show does not sell, the loss approaches the full commitment. That is why the promoter is paid to absorb variance, and why the segment earns **3.29%** while ticketing earns **35.48%** (§14).

The asset it uses is stated in §28: **BookMyShow observes intent before it observes purchase** — notification sign-ups, waitlist joins, reminders and pre-sale registrations accumulate in the window between announcement and on-sale. Crucially, **that window sits before the promoter's financial commitment locks.**

The gap it closes is stated in §30: **the decision to commit money and the data that could price it sit in different functions, and nobody owns the join.**

The Demand Gate is not a new data source and not a new product surface. It is **a decision rule, an owner, and a scoreboard** placed between two things the company already has.

---

## 51. Proposal Mechanics

1. **Classify every event** as *company-risk* (BookMyShow capital committed) or *fee-only*. Only the first category enters the gate. This classification is the denominator of §31 and must exist before anything else works.
2. **At announcement**, the gate produces a **forecast attendance with a stated band**, from the pre-purchase signal in §28 plus comparable-event history.
3. **A published threshold** — set per event tier, not per event — determines pass or fail. It is published internally *before* the forecast is run, so it cannot be fitted to the answer.
4. **Commitment follows the gate**, not the reverse. Failing the gate does not forbid the event; it moves it to fee-only terms, a smaller venue, or a lower guarantee.
5. **Overrides are allowed and recorded.** An override is not a failure of the system; an unrecorded one is.
6. **After the event**, realised attendance is compared to the forecast and the result is reported. This is what makes the gate falsifiable rather than ceremonial.

---

## 52. Proposal Economics

The gate does not raise revenue. It **reduces the variance of the loss-making tail**, which is the only lever that moves a 3.29%-structure business without changing what it sells.

**North Star: GRE/100** (§31). **Guardrail: NAS-90 — New-Artist Share at the 90th percentile of gate-rejection intensity.** In the decile of periods where the gate rejects most, what share of the approved slate is first-time artists?

The guardrail exists because the gate's obvious failure mode is that it works: a threshold built on observed pre-purchase intent will systematically favour artists who already have an audience, and a live business that only stages known names has stopped discovering. **NAS-90 is measured at the 90th percentile precisely because the average will look fine** — the damage happens in the periods when the gate is biting hardest.

NAS-90 is owned by a function with **no revenue target** (§48).

---

## 53. Phase 0 — Two Analyst-Weeks, With Kill Criteria

Before anything is built: **2 analyst-weeks, backtesting 200 past events** against data the company already holds.

The question is narrow. *For events where BookMyShow carried risk, did the pre-on-sale signal predict realised attendance to within ±20%?*

**Kill criteria, stated before the work:**

- **K1 — the signal does not predict.** Pre-on-sale intent fails to forecast attendance to ±20% at usable frequency. The gate has nothing to gate on.
- **K2 — the timing does not work.** *Most likely to fire.* The events that lost money were not predictable from pre-on-sale signal **because the commitment was made before any signal existed.** If the money is committed at booking rather than at announcement, the gate is watching the wrong moment and the fix is a contracting change, not a product.
- **K3 — the categories do not separate.** Company-risk and fee-only events cannot be distinguished in the historical record. Without the denominator, §31 cannot be computed at all.

**K2 is the one I expect to fire**, and it is the reason Phase 0 costs two analyst-weeks rather than a quarter of engineering. If it fires, the entire proposal is wrong in a way no amount of building would have revealed.

---

## 54. Experiment Design & Falsification

If Phase 0 survives, the pilot runs the gate in **shadow mode** on one event tier for two quarters: the gate produces forecasts and pass/fail calls, decisions are made as they are today, and the two are compared. No commitment behaviour changes until the gate has been scored against reality.

**R1 — the promotion threshold:** the gate must improve GRE/100 by **12 percentage points** over the shadow-mode baseline, while the pilot slate holds **at least 25% new artists** (§52). Both conditions, or the gate does not leave shadow mode.

The asymmetry is intentional: a gate that improves GRE/100 by collapsing the slate to safe names has failed, and the 25% floor is what makes that failure visible rather than celebrated.

---

## 55. Early-Warning Indicators

From §32's decomposition: **falling gate coverage** (events bypassing the gate) is the earliest sign of institutional rejection. **Rising override rate** is the second. **Widening forecast bands** indicate the model is being asked about events unlike anything it has seen. **NAS-90 falling** means the gate is working and narrowing the business at the same time.

---

## 56. Roadmap

**Q1 — Phase 0.** 2 analyst-weeks, 200 events, K1–K3 evaluated. Classify company-risk vs fee-only. Gate not built.

**Q2–Q3 — Shadow mode.** Gate runs on one tier, forecasts recorded, no decisions changed. GRE/100 and NAS-90 instrumented. R1 assessed at the end of Q3.

**Q4 — Live on one tier.** Only if R1 clears. Published thresholds, recorded overrides, post-event scoring reported to the board.

**Year 2 — Extension.** Additional tiers only where the Q4 tier's forecast accuracy has held. **No extension on the strength of the idea; only on the strength of the scoreboard.**

## 57. Go-To-Market

The gate is internal and has no external launch. Its only outward-facing consequence is on the artist and promoter side: **a company that can state, with evidence, how accurately it forecasts demand is negotiating from a different position than one that cannot** (§25). That is a commercial asset in a market where guarantees are being bid up (§46, R4).

## 58. Org Changes Required

One change, and it is the hard one: **someone must own the join between the demand signal and the commitment decision** (§30).

The gate's threshold and its scoreboard sit with a function carrying **no revenue target** (§48). The live commercial team keeps every decision right — including the right to override — and loses only the right to override *silently*.

## 59. Success Criteria

**Twelve months:** company-risk events classified and countable; GRE/100 reported monthly; forecast accuracy published post-event; NAS-90 above the **25%** floor.

**Twenty-four months:** contribution variance on company-risk events measurably narrower than the pre-gate baseline; the gate extended only to tiers where accuracy held.

**Not success:** a higher GRE/100 achieved by staging fewer events, or by reclassifying company-risk events as fee-only.

## 65. Appendices

### A-1 — The entity is eight years older than the product, and formally unclassified

**Big Tree Entertainment Private Limited was incorporated in 1999**; the BookMyShow product dates from **2007**. 🟢 The company's **NIC code is 99999 — "unclassified"**. India's industrial classification has no box for a business that sells other people's tickets and stages its own events. It is a small detail with a real consequence: **no official category means no official peer set**, which is part of why §14 has to reach for a New York Stock Exchange filer to find a comparator at all.

### A-2 — PBT and the tax line are derived, not disclosed

Total income (₹1,869 Cr) minus total expenses (₹1,704 Cr) gives **implied PBT of ₹165 Cr**; against PAT of ₹192 Cr this implies a **₹27 Cr credit**. FY24: ₹1,430 − ₹1,320 = **₹110 Cr** against PAT of ₹109 Cr, a **₹1 Cr charge** and an effective rate of **0.91%**.

**Two honest caveats.** First, "total income" may not be the exact statutory base for PBT, so the derived figures carry that uncertainty. Second, **a deferred-tax credit is entirely normal for a company turning durably profitable** — recognising previously unrecognised losses is what the accounting is for. The claim here is not that anything is irregular. It is narrower and it stands: **the ₹27 Cr is 14.06% of reported PAT, it is not an operating gain, and it is unlikely to repeat.** The operating result grew **50.00%**; the headline says **76.15%**.

The signature is visible without any of the arithmetic above: **FY25 PAT margin (10.27%) exceeds FY25 PBT margin (8.83%).** A company cannot earn more after tax than before it on operations alone.

### A-3 — The KKR amount is a range, and the range is 25% wide

Coverage reports **$40–50 Mn** for 6%. The spread between the two figures is **25%**, and it propagates: implied valuation **$666.7–833.3 Mn**, **₹6,333–7,917 Cr**, **33.0×–41.2× FY25 PAT**. No single point estimate is used anywhere in this case study, and none should be.

### A-4 — Live Nation's ticketing margin: 35.48% computed, "37%" as described

One source characterises Live Nation's ticketing margin as roughly 37%. Computed from the segment figures in Reference 3 — $1,100 Mn AOI on $3,100 Mn revenue — it is **35.48%**. **The computed figure is used throughout**, and the difference is noted rather than reconciled, since the basis of the 37% characterisation is not stated. The argument does not turn on it: at either figure the gap to concerts' 3.29% is an order of magnitude.
