# IndiGo — The Cost Base That Grows At 13% No Matter What You Do

> Everyone read IndiGo's Q1 FY27 loss as a fuel shock, and the fuel line supports them: ₹10,832.9 Cr, up 85.7%, an increase of ₹5,000.3 Cr that on its own exceeded the entire ₹4,080.3 Cr increase in revenue. But IndiGo discloses a unit cost with neither fuel nor currency in it, and that number has gone +3.8% (FY26), +7.3% (Q4 FY26), +10.7% (Q1 FY27) — accelerating for three consecutive reported periods. Multiply each by the capacity it was spread over and the total ex-fuel, ex-FX cost grew 13.66%, 10.95% and 13.91%: essentially flat at ~13% while capacity growth collapsed from 9.5% to 2.9%. IndiGo's cost base grows at a rate set by its order book. Its capacity grows at a rate set by demand it neither controls nor forecasts, because with 67.4% of India's domestic market its only demand instrument is the spot fare, sold one flight at a time to an anonymous buyer. Fuel did not cause this quarter. Fuel made it legible.

## 1. Executive Summary

IndiGo reported a net loss of **₹238.0 Cr** for the quarter ended 30 June 2026 on revenue from operations of **₹24,584.1 Cr, up 19.9%**. The near-universal reading was a fuel and currency shock interrupting a healthy business — a reading management endorsed, describing the quarter as "near-term cost volatility rather than structural demand weakness." 🟢

Three disclosed facts complicate it.

**This was not the first loss.** IndiGo lost ₹2,393.6 Cr across FY26 and ₹2,536.9 Cr in Q4 FY26 alone, before the fuel spike. Cost per available seat-kilometre has exceeded revenue per available seat-kilometre in all three of its most recently reported periods — by ₹0.01 across FY26, ₹0.48 in Q4 FY26, ₹0.05 in Q1 FY27. 🟢

**Q1 FY27 was the best of the three.** The gap narrowed 43 paise sequentially; the fuel-shock quarter had materially better unit economics than the one before it, because a 21.3% yield increase did enormous work. 🟢

**And the line with neither fuel nor currency in it is accelerating.** CASK excluding fuel and foreign exchange rose **3.8% (FY26), 7.3% (Q4 FY26), 10.7% (Q1 FY27)** — 2.82× the full-year rate in the latest quarter. 🟢 Reverse each against the capacity it was spread over and implied *total* ex-fuel, ex-FX cost growth is **13.66%, 10.95%, 13.91%**: a 2.96-point spread across periods whose capacity growth spread 6.6 points, 2.23× wider. **IndiGo's controllable cost base grows at roughly 13% a year almost regardless of how much it flies.** Unit cost is that 13% divided by a denominator demand sets — and ASK growth has gone 9.5% → 3.4% → 2.9%, with Q2 FY27 guided flat. Domestic traffic fell 4.8% year on year in July 2026; the January–July market grew 0.64%. IndiGo carries 67.4% of it.

The product question is therefore not "how do we cut cost" but **why does the largest airline in one of the world's largest aviation markets have no forward visibility into its own demand?** Each of the 3.13 crore passengers carried in the quarter was an independent spot transaction. IndiGo's corporate programme, per its own published terms, is a discount on spot fares with no prepayment, no committed volume, no guaranteed inventory and no price band. 🟢 Its loyalty programme last published enrolment of 2 million against 12.34 crore annual passengers. 🟡 And ancillary revenue — the one line that is a product decision rather than a fare pass-through — grew at **59.22% of the rate the fare grew** and *fell* as a share of revenue.

This case study proposes **6E Forward**, a prepaid corridor-scoped seat entitlement converting a fraction of spot demand into contracted demand visible when the schedule is frozen. North Star: **CBA/1k — Committed Block ASKs per 1,000 *planned* ASKs at schedule freeze**, denominated in planned capacity so adding unsold capacity lowers it. Guardrail: **SDA-90 — Spot Displacement at the 90th percentile**. Under a stress test built from IndiGo's own disclosure — traffic growth as a fraction of revenue growth, 1.4% ÷ 19.9% = **7.04%** — 6E Forward falls from 3rd of 4 to last, behind a fleet initiative this case study did not propose. It is kept in anyway, and §53 names the kill criterion most likely to fire.

## 3. Why This Product, Why Now

Aviation was the last untouched category in this series and, for 59 days, the least appealing: "airline loses money on fuel" is not an insight.

Two things changed that. **Day 59.** That case study found ixigo's disclosed gross take rate on flights compressing 9.17% → 8.41% in the same quarter, which management attributed to post-conflict fare inflation. Day 60 is the other side of that transaction: the airline that *set* those fares raised yields 21.3% and still lost money. Two independently-reporting companies, neither controlling the other, describing one macro event from opposite ends — a stronger evidentiary base than either disclosure alone, and the second consecutive time this series has found the pattern useful.

**And IndiGo's disclosure is unusually good.** It publishes CASK excluding fuel *and* foreign exchange, which most carriers do not break out, so the "it was fuel" hypothesis can be tested against the company's own numbers rather than argued about. It reported that figure three times in fifteen months. The three readings are the case study.

## 5. Product Overview

IndiGo is a low-cost carrier flying a single-aisle fleet point-to-point, operating **432 aircraft** to **97 domestic and 46 international destinations** on up to **2,298 daily flights** as at 30 June 2026. 🟢 The passenger-facing product is deliberately narrow — a fare, a seat, a bag allowance, and à-la-carte add-ons sold at booking, joined in Q1 FY27 by "light fares" for baggage-free travellers with add-ons priced separately. 🟢 The strategic product surface is the schedule itself, decided months ahead and the single largest determinant of both revenue and cost; this case study argues it is made with far less information than it could be.


---
## 6. History & Timeline

Incorporated January 2004, first flight August 2006, IPO November 2015. IndiGo grew through the 2010s on one aircraft type, high utilisation, punctuality as the brand promise and relentless domestic capacity addition, absorbing the collapse of Kingfisher, Jet Airways and Go First to reach **67.4% of domestic passengers** by mid-2026 — the highest share any carrier has held in the modern Indian market. 🟢 The recent chapter is different in character: FY26 total income ₹89,513.4 Cr (+6.4%) but a **net loss of ₹2,393.6 Cr** against a prior-year profit of ₹7,258.4 Cr, followed by a ₹2,536.9 Cr loss in Q4 FY26 and ₹238.0 Cr in Q1 FY27. 🟢


---
## 7. Market Context

Indian domestic aviation carried **1.20 crore passengers in July 2026, down 4.80% year on year**, with January–July 2026 traffic at **984.03 lakh against 977.79 lakh** — growth of **0.64%**. 🟢 July ran **14.64% below** the seven-month monthly average of 1.41 crore, partly monsoon seasonality and partly not. IndiGo's capacity guidance of flat Q2 and single-digit FY27 growth is usually read as caution about cost; it is at least as much a statement about demand, because a market growing 0.64% cannot absorb double-digit capacity growth from the carrier already holding two-thirds of it.


---
## 8. Regulatory Context

The dominant regulatory fact of the quarter is that **the price IndiGo pays for domestic jet fuel is administered, not market-set.**

From **1 April 2026** the Government capped the increase in domestic ATF prices at **25%** plus taxes in response to a global fuel shock. 🟢 On **7 June 2026** the cap was replaced by fixed benchmarks — **₹86.32/litre FOB domestic, ₹104.49/litre international** — translating after charges and taxes to roughly ₹115/litre at Delhi, ₹114.50 at Mumbai and ₹139 at Chennai. 🟢 Oil marketing companies, reportedly absorbing under-recoveries near **₹30/litre**, received a one-time interest-free advance of up to **₹10,000 Cr**, with compensation **recoverable once international prices moderate**. 🟢

Three consequences. **The quarter was 75.82% capped** — 69 of 91 days fell before 8 June, at a blended ATF rate of roughly ₹140/litre. 🟡 The relief is **prospective and recoverable**, a timing instrument rather than a subsidy. And it covers domestic operations only, which is the seam §39 runs the Five Forces across. Airfare caps have separately been imposed episodically in this market and remain a standing political possibility after a 21.3% yield rise.


---
## 9. Problem Statement

**For a product organisation:** IndiGo commits to a cost base — aircraft, engines, crew, slots, maintenance, ground handling — years in advance, then discovers what demand will be for that capacity somewhere between ninety days and ninety minutes before departure. The gap between committed cost growth (~13% a year) and realised capacity growth (2.9% and falling) is the unit-cost problem, and it is a *forecasting and commitment* problem wearing a cost problem's clothes. The instrument used to close it is the spot fare; in Q1 FY27 it was used hard, and 21.3% of yield recovered 16.5% of RASK against 32.6% of CASK growth at a cost of 1.3 points of load factor.

**For a passenger:** the traveller who flies Delhi–Bengaluru eleven times a year buys eleven auctions, each at whatever the algorithm says that day, with the price rising exactly when they most need to travel. There is no version of *"I will fly this route roughly monthly; give me a price and I will commit."* Those two are the same problem seen from both ends.


---
## 10. Who Actually Pays

Q1 FY27 revenue from operations of ₹24,584.1 Cr breaks into **passenger ticket revenue ₹21,878.6 Cr** (+23.0%), **ancillary revenue ₹2,453.4 Cr** (+13.9%), and a derived residual of **₹252.1 Cr**, 1.03% of the total — §63 explains why that residual is not used for anything. 🟢 Ninety percent of the money comes from a person or their employer buying a seat; ten percent comes from everything IndiGo's product organisation actually designs. That ratio is the ceiling on how much product work can matter, and it moved the wrong way this quarter.

## 11. User Personas

*Constructed from disclosed operating data and stated network strategy, not primary research — see ASSUMPTIONS Part 3.*

**The high-frequency domestic commuter** flies a few trunk sectors repeatedly, often booked through an employer's travel desk, sensitive to schedule and punctuality more than headline fare, and structurally exposed to fare volatility because they travel on dates they do not choose. This is who 6E Forward is for, and who IndiGo currently monetises identically to a once-a-year leisure flyer.

**The price-elastic leisure traveller** books far ahead, chooses dates around price, and will not fly above a threshold — the persona that disappeared this quarter, which a 21.3% yield rise against 0.7% passenger growth in a market down 4.8% is what looks like in aggregate.

**The SME traveller** is a small business whose two or three people fly regularly: too small for a negotiated contract, too frequent to behave like leisure. **The international connecting passenger** is served increasingly by A321XLRs and damp-leased widebodies toward a 40%-international-ASK target, with a different competitive set and different fuel economics — which is why §39 treats them separately.


---
## 12. User Journey

Search, compare, book, add on, check in, fly — a short journey the product executes well, at 99.9% technical dispatch reliability and 86.9% on-time performance. 🟢 What matters is what the journey does *not* contain: no moment at which a traveller expresses future intent to IndiGo, and no moment at which IndiGo offers anything in exchange for it. Search happens close to travel, booking is transactional and terminal, add-ons are offered inside the booking flow next to a fare that just rose 22% (§25), and loyalty accrues after the fact — nothing is forward-looking, so nothing helps the schedule get made.


---
## 13. TAM / SAM / SOM — Restricted Form

*Framework note: run in restricted form. No primary-sourced market-size figure for Indian aviation could be verified to this series' standard, so the opportunity is sized from IndiGo's own disclosed base — the default since Day 57.*

IndiGo carried **12.34 crore passengers in FY26**: not a market estimate, an actual disclosed count of people who flew IndiGo in twelve months. That is the addressable base for any product change.

Segmenting it requires an assumption, and the honest move is to name it. If the high-frequency cohort — six or more IndiGo trips a year — is on the order of a fifth of passengers, that is roughly 2.5 crore passenger-journeys and around ₹1.75 lakh Cr of ticket revenue at Q1 FY27's ₹6,990 average. **That fraction is author-constructed and unverified** (Assumption A4); IndiGo does not publish trip-frequency distribution.

The obtainable share is better anchored to something real: IndiGo's most recent published opt-in figure for a voluntary programme — BluChip, 2 million members — is **1.62% of FY26 passengers**. 🟡 A commitment product demanding more effort than joining a free loyalty scheme should not be expected to beat that early. §47 turns this into a stress rule.

## 14. Competitive Landscape — Restricted To Filers

*Framework note: restricted to competitors whose numbers are filed. Air India and Air India Express file with the MCA and reported FY26 on 28 July 2026. **SpiceJet is listed and files, and SNV Aviation (Akasa Air) files with the MCA — both are named rather than silently ignored, but neither had reported for the quarter ended 30 June 2026 at the time of writing, so neither is deep-dived and no estimate is constructed for them.***

**Air India and Air India Express, FY26 combined: revenue ₹71,870 Cr, net loss ₹22,238 Cr** — more than double FY25's ₹10,859 Cr, on revenue down roughly 9%. Air India standalone contributed ₹51,452 Cr and ₹15,368 Cr; Air India Express ₹19,088 Cr and ₹6,767 Cr. 🟢 The entity figures sum to ₹70,540 Cr and ₹22,135 Cr against the stated group totals, leaving residuals of ₹1,330 Cr and ₹103 Cr — consolidation adjustments or other entities, not reconciled here (Appendix A-2). Chairman N. Chandrasekaran called the turnaround "a five- to 10-year journey."

The comparison cuts against the easy story. IndiGo's FY26 loss margin was **2.82%** of revenue from operations; Air India group's was **30.94%** — **10.98× IndiGo's, on 15.4% less revenue**, and **9.29×** the absolute loss.

So IndiGo is not losing a competitive fight. It is winning one decisively — taking share while its largest competitor's revenue *fell* — and still not covering its costs, with the two carriers between them booking **₹24,631.6 Cr of FY26 losses** in a market growing 0.64%. If the problem were competitive, executing better against Air India would fix it. It is structural to the price-cost equation of flying Indians around India, and the fix has to change the equation rather than win harder.

## 15. Business Model

IndiGo sells seat-kilometres and buys aircraft time, fuel, crew, slots, ground handling and maintenance, with ancillary services attached to the seat transaction; there is no subscription, no committed-volume contract, no forward sale and no material recurring revenue line. The model's defining property is that **cost is committed and revenue is discovered** — an aircraft ordered in 2022 arrives in 2026 with a fifteen-year lease attached, and the fare it flies on is set the week before departure. Every airline lives with this; what varies is how much of the gap they close with instruments other than the spot fare, and IndiGo's disclosed answer on the demand side is essentially none.

## 16. Unit Economics

*Framework note: the standard LCC unit-economics frame (RASK vs CASK) is used unmodified, because IndiGo publishes both plus a fuel-and-FX-stripped variant, which is the rare case where the company's own disclosure is better than any reconstruction.*

This is the section the case study turns on.

| Period | RASK (₹) | CASK (₹) | Gap (₹/ASK) | CASK ex-fuel, ex-FX (₹) | ...growth | ASK growth |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FY26 (full year) | 4.99 | 5.00 | **−0.01** | 3.00 | **+3.8%** | +9.5% |
| Q4 FY26 | 5.30 | 5.78 | **−0.48** | 3.15 | **+7.3%** | +3.4% |
| Q1 FY27 | 5.66 | 5.71 | **−0.05** | 3.20 | **+10.7%** | +2.9% |

Three readings, all from IndiGo's own results releases. 🟢

**First: cost per seat-kilometre has exceeded revenue per seat-kilometre in all three.** IndiGo lost money on every seat-kilometre it flew, in the full year, in the quarter before this one, and in this one. On Q1 FY27's 43.5 billion ASK, the five-paise gap is ₹217.5 Cr.

**Second: Q1 FY27 was the best of the three.** The gap narrowed 43 paise sequentially, to 10.42% of Q4 FY26's. The quarter everyone described as a fuel disaster had materially better unit economics than the quarter before it, because the 21.3% yield increase worked. That single fact should make anyone suspicious of the fuel narrative.

**Third — the thesis: the ex-fuel, ex-FX line is accelerating.** +3.8%, then +7.3% (1.92× the full-year rate), then +10.7% (1.47× that, and 2.82× the full-year rate). Neither Brent nor the rupee is in that number.

Now reverse it. Unit cost is total cost divided by capacity, so implied total ex-fuel, ex-FX cost growth is (1 + capacity growth) × (1 + unit cost growth) − 1:

- FY26: 1.095 × 1.038 → **+13.66%**
- Q4 FY26: 1.034 × 1.073 → **+10.95%**
- Q1 FY27: 1.029 × 1.107 → **+13.91%**

**A 2.96-percentage-point spread in total cost growth across three periods whose capacity growth spread 6.6 points — 2.23× wider.** The cost base grows at about 13% whatever capacity does. Unit cost is that 13% divided by a denominator set by demand.

*A caveat stated plainly, because it matters.* One of the three readings is a full year and two are quarters; quarterly figures carry seasonality that an annual figure averages away, so this is not a like-for-like time series. The claim being made is about the *level* of total cost growth being insensitive to capacity, not about a smooth trend. Assumption A1 in ASSUMPTIONS treats this as the load-bearing weak point and gives the rival reading equal air.

## 17. Revenue Architecture

Q1 FY27 revenue from operations ₹24,584.1 Cr (+19.9%); total income ₹25,614.1 Cr (+18.9%), growing slower, so non-operating income shrank as a share — the inverse of Day 59's finding at ixigo, where treasury income supplied 85% of profit. The decomposition is the story: RASK grew 16.5% and ASK 2.9%, which compounded reproduces 19.88% of the disclosed 19.9%, while traffic grew 1.4% and passengers carried 0.7%. Expressed as the ratio this file reuses in §47, **traffic growth was 7.04% of revenue growth** — not a decomposition, but an honest measure of how much of IndiGo's growth came from people flying more rather than paying more, and the answer is almost none of it.

## 18. Cost Architecture

Q1 FY27 total expenses **₹25,852.5 Cr, up 34.4%** against revenue up 19.9%, including fuel ₹10,832.9 Cr, maintenance and repairs ₹3,497.5 Cr (14.23% of revenue from operations) and depreciation and amortisation ₹2,970.2 Cr (12.08%). 🟢

Two reconciliation checks worth publishing. Fuel CASK ₹2.49 plus ex-fuel CASK ₹3.22 equals the disclosed CASK of ₹5.71 exactly — but ₹5.71 × 43.5 bn ASK is ₹24,838.5 Cr, **₹1,014.0 Cr short of total expenses, 3.92% of them.** IndiGo's CASK therefore excludes a cost line of roughly that size, most plausibly net finance cost on a ₹53,755.6 Cr operating lease liability; **this is derived as a residual, not disclosed**, and flagged rather than asserted (Appendix A-3). Note it means CASK *understates* the full cost per seat-kilometre by about four paise, which **widens** §16's RASK–CASK gap rather than narrowing it. The second check holds cleanly: ex-fuel CASK ₹3.22 less the ₹82.5 Cr FX loss spread over 43.5 bn ASK (₹0.019) rounds to the disclosed ex-fuel ex-FX ₹3.20, so the two definitions are internally consistent.


---
## 19. The Fuel Line

Fuel expense rose ₹5,832.6 Cr → **₹10,832.9 Cr, up 85.7%** — an increase of **₹5,000.3 Cr** against a revenue increase of ₹4,080.3 Cr. **The fuel increase alone was 122.55% of the entire revenue increase**, a ₹920.0 Cr shortfall before any other cost moved, taking fuel from 28.45% to 44.07% of revenue from operations. Fuel CASK rose 80.4% to ₹2.49, with Brent up roughly 50% and Singapore jet fuel up nearly 120% year on year. 🟢

And the fact that should govern how the next two quarters are read: **75.82% of the quarter — 69 of 91 days — fell under the government's 25% cap.** Q2 FY27 has no cap at all, and the relief is **recoverable**, so whatever benefit the quarter received is a timing difference rather than a gift.


---
## 20. The Currency Line

The rupee moved 85.29 → 95.02 against the dollar, an **11.41% depreciation**. 🟢 IndiGo disclosed EBITDAR of ₹3,832.5 Cr against ₹4,064.9 Cr excluding FX — a **₹232.4 Cr** impact — and a net loss of ₹238.0 Cr against ₹5.6 Cr excluding FX, again **₹232.4 Cr**: identical to the rupee, meaning **the entire foreign-exchange impact sits above the EBITDAR line** as operating-cost translation, not a below-the-line revaluation. Excluding forex the quarter lost ₹5.6 Cr on ₹24,584.1 Cr of revenue, or 0.02% — exactly breakeven, which is another way of saying that the largest fuel spike in years met the largest price response in IndiGo's history and produced breakeven at a carrier holding 67.4% of its market.


---
## 21. Capacity & Network

Capacity 43.5 billion ASK (**+2.9%**), traffic 36.2 billion RPK (**+1.4%**), load factor **83.3%, down 1.3 points** — the 1.5-point gap between capacity and traffic growth is the arithmetic of seats added that did not sell. 🟢 Guidance is the important part: **Q2 FY27 flat capacity with PRASK growth above 25%, FY27 single-digit ASK growth**, rising to early double digits through FY30. 🟢 Read against §16, flat capacity means the ~13% cost base is divided by a denominator that does not grow at all, so Q2 ex-fuel CASK growth should exceed Q1's 10.7% — which is what IndiGo's own "single-digit to early double-digit" cost guidance implies.


---
## 22. Fleet

Closing fleet **432 aircraft**: 13 inducted, 9 original-order and 13 damp-leased redelivered — a net reduction of 9 from FY26's closing 441 and an increase of 16 on the prior-year quarter, both true of different bases and a frequent source of confusion in coverage (Appendix A-4). 🟢 Additions included 3 A321 freighters and 3 A321XLRs against a fleet containing 175 A321neos, with over 1,000 LEAP-1A engines contracted from CFM International, while **older A320ceos are being parked because fuel prices make them uneconomic**. 🟡 That last item is a pure cost decision requiring no traveller to do anything differently, which is precisely why §47 uses fleet transition as the stress-exempt initiative — and why it wins.


---
## 23. Demand

IndiGo carried 3.13 crore passengers, up 0.7%, in a domestic market that shrank 4.8% in July, with load factor down 1.3 points despite capacity growing only 2.9%; May 2026 was nonetheless a landmark month at over 10 million domestic passengers. 🟡 Management's position is that this reflects near-term cost volatility rather than structural demand weakness, and that capacity will ramp in the Q3 peak — a reasonable reading that may well be right. The alternative, which the same numbers support, is that a 21.3% fare increase in a price-elastic market removed the marginal traveller, and that the increase was not optional.


---
## 24. Pricing & Revenue Management

Yield **₹6.04 (+21.3%)**, PRASK **₹5.03 (+19%)**, RASK ₹5.66 (+16.5%); yield × load factor reproduces PRASK to 0.03%, an internal check that passes. 🟢 The three rates stepping down is the mechanical record of a price increase leaking value at each stage — some given back in load factor, and ancillary growing slower than fares so total unit revenue grew slower still. Q2 guidance of PRASK above 25% on flat capacity presses the same lever into a shrinking market with the fuel cap gone; it may be the only available move, but it is not one that can be repeated a third time.


---
## 25. Ancillary & Attach

This is the product organisation's section, and the bridge from a cost thesis to a product proposal.

Ancillary revenue was **₹2,453.4 Cr, up 13.9%**, against passenger ticket revenue up 23.0%. Per passenger: ancillary **₹783.83 (+13.11%)**, ticket **₹6,990.00 (+22.14%)** — **ancillary per passenger grew at 59.22% of the rate the fare grew** — and ancillary fell from **10.51% to 9.98% of revenue from operations**. 🟢 This happened in the quarter IndiGo *launched* light fares, the exact mechanism designed to raise attach.

Two readings, and this file does not claim to separate them. **Mix:** a 22% higher fare may have shifted the passenger mix toward business travellers whose employers already cover bags. **Anchoring:** the add-on is offered inside the booking flow, immediately after a fare 22% higher than the traveller remembers — a ₹600 bag next to a ₹5,723 fare and the same ₹600 bag next to a ₹6,990 fare are not the same decision. Only the second has a testable implication, which is why §54 tests it. Either way the fact stands: **the revenue line IndiGo's product teams control converted the fare increase at 0.59 to 1**, and shrank as a share of the business, exactly when it was needed most.


---
## 26. Loyalty

BluChip launched in 2024 and **passed 2 million members within roughly six months**, as reported in March 2025 — the most recent enrolment figure verifiable here, and **1.62%** of FY26's 12.34 crore passengers. 🟡 The programme has almost certainly grown; the point is that IndiGo has not published a newer number, so 1.62% is a stale floor, and an Accor ALL partnership went live during Q1 FY27 extending earn and burn across hotels. 🟡 Structurally, BluChip is *retrospective* — it rewards flying that already happened, creates a mild preference for the next booking, and generates nothing IndiGo can use when the schedule is frozen ninety days out.

## 31. North Star Metric

**CBA/1k — Committed Block ASKs per 1,000 planned ASKs, measured at schedule freeze.**

The denominator is the design choice, and it is the most reusable technique in this series. It is **planned** ASK, taken when the schedule locks — conventionally around ninety days out — not ASK flown and not seats sold. Add capacity you have not sold forward and CBA/1k *falls*: the metric punishes exactly the behaviour that created §16's problem.

An ASK counts only if all four hold, conjunctively: it was **paid for before the schedule freeze** under a committed entitlement; it was **flown by the committing party**, not resold, refunded or expired; it flew at a **realised fare inside the contracted band**, so bands cannot be written wide enough to be meaningless; and there was **no involuntary downgrade, denied boarding or reaccommodation** on that segment.

Condition 2 is what makes it honest — a prepaid entitlement people buy and never use is deferred revenue, pleasant for cash flow and useless for schedule planning, and this metric refuses to count it. Condition 4 ties the North Star to the guardrail: a committed seat IndiGo cannot honour subtracts from its own numerator.


---
## 32. Supporting Metric Tree

CBA/1k decomposes into four inputs a team can own separately: **eligible-traveller penetration**, **entitlement depth** (ASK committed per holder per period), **realisation** (share of committed ASK actually flown by the holder), and **band integrity** (share of committed segments flown inside the contracted fare band). Two of these fail in opposite directions and should be watched as a pair: high penetration with low realisation means IndiGo has sold options nobody exercises, which is a product failure dressed as commercial success; high realisation with low penetration means the product works but nobody knows about it, which is a distribution problem and much cheaper to fix.

## 33. Jobs To Be Done

*Framework note: JTBD stated for the high-frequency traveller rather than a generic flyer, because the proposal is deliberately not for everyone.*

**"When I know I will need to fly this route repeatedly over the next six months but not exactly when, help me stop being exposed to whatever the fare is on the day I find out, so my travel decisions stop being hostage to a price I cannot predict."** The current product does not do this job at all — booking early is not a solution when the traveller does not know the dates, and a refundable fare is expensive insurance against a price that has already risen, so there is nothing between "book now for a specific date" and "pay whatever it costs later." The mirror job, for IndiGo: *"When I am deciding what to fly in November, help me know how much of it is already sold."*

## 34. Kano Analysis

Punctuality is a **must-be** attribute at IndiGo and produces no delight at 99.9% reliability; fare level is **one-dimensional**, which is why raising it 21.3% cost load factor; seat selection, meals and priority boarding are **indifferent** for most passengers, a plausible structural explanation for why attach did not follow the fare up (§25). Price *certainty* over a horizon is the interesting cell — it is **absent** rather than indifferent, because no Indian carrier offers it and so no traveller has revealed a preference about it. Kano cannot classify an attribute nobody has been offered, which is an argument for testing it cheaply (§54) and against confidence (§47).

## 35. Feature Inventory

The relevant inventory item is IndiGo's **6E corporate programme**, because the proposal in §50 would be redundant if it already existed.

## 36. Feature Gaps

The gap is a single missing primitive: **a forward commitment instrument.** Everything IndiGo sells is either a specific seat on a specific flight or a discount on the price of one; nothing lets a traveller or business buy *capacity* rather than a *flight*, so IndiGo has no demand information earlier than a booking curve that starts far too late to inform a schedule, and no revenue line with any duration to it. A secondary gap from §25: add-ons are sold at the fare moment, anchored against a number that just moved 22%, with no decoupled purchase surface — and §47 scores fixing that higher than the proposal this case study actually makes.

## 37. Switching Costs & Moat

IndiGo's moat is real and it is a **distribution and schedule moat** — 67.4% domestic share, 2,298 daily flights, 97 domestic destinations, and trunk-route frequency no competitor matches. ## 40. Trust & Safety

**§40 is placed before §50 in this series whenever the proposal creates a harmful incentive. It does here, and the reorder is retained.**

**Displacement.** Inventory protected for committed holders is inventory unavailable to walk-up buyers. Protecting 15 seats on a peak-day Delhi–Patna departure means 15 fewer in the spot pool, on a route where the marginal traveller may be flying for a family emergency at a fare they can barely meet. A commitment product that transfers price certainty to the frequent business traveller by taking availability from the infrequent one has made the market worse.

**Forfeiture.** Prepaid entitlements that expire unused are revenue for the airline and pure loss for the buyer, and the temptation to design expiry aggressively is exactly as strong as it looks. §51 therefore fixes expiry and forfeiture as **contractual pre-commitments set before launch**, not parameters a revenue team can tune.

**Guardrail: SDA-90 — Spot Displacement at the 90th percentile.** On the decile of departures with the highest commitment penetration, the change in lowest-available spot fare and spot seats offered against matched controls. Owned by a **Passenger Access function with no revenue target**, reporting outside commercial. Allocation logs **architecturally firewalled from revenue management**, enforced by a build-pipeline test rather than a policy document. Two consecutive weeks of breach automatically suspends new commitment sales on the affected routes.

## 41. Accessibility & Inclusion

The displacement risk in §40 is an equity risk before it is a metrics risk: high-frequency travellers skew urban and salaried, and the marginal price-elastic traveller on a tier-2 route does not. A product that stabilises price for the first group by tightening availability for the second is regressive even if every individual transaction is voluntary. SDA-90 is therefore measured **by route tier, not in aggregate**, with tier-2 and tier-3 reported separately — an aggregate number would let a strong trunk-route result hide a bad one where it matters most, the same reasoning behind the 90th-percentile guardrails on Days 57, 58 and 59.

## 48. Incentive Design

Three incentives are excluded by design, permanently, because each would convert the proposal into the harm §40 describes.

**No revenue-management access to commitment-holder identity or booking history.** Enforced by the build-pipeline firewall in §40, not by policy. A yield system that can see who is contractually locked in will price around them.

**No sales or commercial incentive tied to entitlement sell-through.** Compensation on forward-commitment volume produces pressure to sell entitlements to travellers who will not use them, which inflates deferred revenue, fails North Star condition 2, and is a consumer-harm mechanism.

**No expiry or forfeiture parameter under commercial control.** Set contractually before launch, changeable only with the Passenger Access function's sign-off. This is the single most likely place for the product to quietly become predatory.

## 49. What I Would Not Build

**Not a subscription** — a flat monthly fee for flights inverts the economics so the airline wins when you fly less, which is the wrong shape for a business whose problem is knowing how much capacity to plan. **Not an international version**, per §45. **Not a fare-freeze guarantee**, because a promise to hold a price without a matching commitment from the buyer is an option written for free, and options written for free get exercised precisely when they hurt most. **Not a points multiplier** — BluChip already does retrospective reward, and more of it creates no forward information, which is the entire point.

## 50. Feature Proposal — 6E Forward

**6E Forward is a prepaid, corridor-scoped seat entitlement that converts a fraction of IndiGo's spot demand into contracted demand visible at the moment the schedule is frozen.**

A traveller or small business buys a block of segments on a named corridor — a route-pair or small cluster, not the network — for a defined forward window, at a fixed price band agreed at purchase, redeemable up to a stated cut-off against a protected inventory allocation. IndiGo receives cash up front and, far more importantly, **a demand curve by corridor by month that exists before the schedule is locked.**

The case rests on §16 and §35 together: §16 establishes that the unit-cost problem is a denominator problem — a ~13% cost base divided by capacity demand will not support — and §35 establishes, from IndiGo's own published terms, that nothing in the current product set produces forward demand information. The proposal is deliberately narrow: domestic only (§49), corridor-scoped rather than network-wide, aimed at the high-frequency cohort. Its ambition is not to change how India flies, but to make a measurable share of IndiGo's demand knowable ninety days out — and to find out cheaply (§53) whether that share can ever be large enough to matter.


---
## 51. Proposal Mechanics

**Unit of sale:** a block of N segments on a named corridor over a defined forward window. The corridor, not the flight, is the unit — which is what makes commitment possible for a traveller who knows the route but not the date.

**Price:** a band, not a point — a floor and ceiling agreed at purchase, with the realised fare inside it. A band rather than a fixed price because a fixed price is an option written for free (§49), and because a band leaves IndiGo some yield upside while removing the traveller's tail risk, which is the part they actually care about.

**Inventory:** a protected allocation per departure, sized per corridor and capped as a share of seats, with the cap set by the Passenger Access function rather than commercial (§40).

**Redemption:** guaranteed inside the allocation up to a stated cut-off; standard spot terms beyond it. **Expiry and forfeiture:** fixed contractually before launch (§48) — the specific terms are a decision for a real product team with real legal input, and this file deliberately does not invent them. **Non-transferable**, per North Star condition 2, because a tradeable entitlement is a secondary market in seats.

**Corporate variant:** the same instrument sold against a company, with segments drawn by named employees. This is the version most likely to work, because a business budgets forward as a matter of course and an individual mostly does not.


---
## 52. Proposal Economics

The mechanism is not that 6E Forward raises price — it explicitly does not, since the band is agreed in advance. It is that **it changes the denominator's reliability.** Capacity planned against contracted demand carries a higher expected load factor than capacity planned against a forecast, and load factor is the multiplier between yield and PRASK (§24). It also permits something IndiGo currently cannot do meaningfully: **hedge fuel against known block hours**, since an airline that has pre-sold defined flying on defined corridors over a defined window has a hedgeable exposure and one that discovers demand at the booking curve does not.

This file does not model the size of either effect and will not, because doing so needs a trip-frequency distribution IndiGo does not publish (§13, §63). **The mechanism is clear, the magnitude is unknown, and §53 finds the magnitude for two analyst-weeks before anyone builds anything.** The downside shape is statable: if penetration lands in the 1.62–7.04% band implied by §47's stress rule, the effect on an ₹85,000 Cr revenue base is small — which is not a reason to skip the test, but a reason to make it cheap.


---
## 53. Phase 0 — Two Analyst-Weeks, With Kill Criteria

Phase 0 builds nothing. It runs on data IndiGo already holds (§29): booking history by traveller across sectors, for the top 20 domestic corridors by revenue, over eight quarters.

**K1 — the demand isn't repetitive enough.** If fewer than 15% of passengers on the pilot corridors flew that corridor four or more times in the preceding twelve months, there is no cohort to sell to.

**K2 — named as most likely to fire.** If the cohort that *does* fly repeatedly already books far enough ahead to capture low fares, it has no price-risk problem and 6E Forward solves a pain it does not have. The 21.3% yield increase is an average; if it fell disproportionately on infrequent late-booking travellers, the entire premise inverts. This is the proposal's honest weak point, and it is checkable in existing data in days.

**K3 — protected inventory cannot be carved without displacement.** If simulating a 10% allocation against historical booking curves shows spot availability falling at peak on more than 15 of the 20 corridors, the §40 harm is structural rather than manageable. Threshold: **usable results on at least 15 of 20 corridors (75%)**, or the analysis is extended rather than waved through. Any of the three firing stops the work; **K2 is the one to bet on.**


---
## 54. Experiment Design & Falsification

**Arm A — control:** existing product and corporate programme.

**Arm B — the cheap imitation, and the real test:** a **fixed-price advance-purchase fare** on the same corridors — buy now, nominate the date later within the window, no protected inventory, no band, no relationship. Deliverable in weeks on existing systems, and it delivers price certainty *without* the forward-visibility apparatus that is 6E Forward's entire justification. **If Arm B performs comparably, the apparatus is theatre: ship Arm B and stop.**

**Arm C — full 6E Forward** on the pilot corridors: corridor blocks, price bands, protected allocation, corporate variant.

**Pre-registered decision rule R1.** Arm C proceeds beyond pilot only if it beats Arm B by more than **8 percentage points on CBA/1k across two full booking cycles including at least one non-peak month**, with **SDA-90 no worse than baseline on tier-2 and tier-3 routes measured separately** (§41). The threshold is high on purpose: Arm C costs roughly 5× Arm B in effort (§47), and a marginal win does not justify a multiple in cost.

**A separable test for §25.** The anchoring hypothesis is testable independently and far more cheaply — move the add-on purchase surface to a post-booking window, 24 to 72 hours after ticketing, and measure attach against control. That is §47's top-ranked initiative, it does not need 6E Forward to exist, and it should run first regardless.

## 57. Go-To-Market

The corporate variant goes through travel management companies and expense platforms (§44), because that is where corporate travel is actually approved; the individual variant, if it ever ships, goes directly to the high-frequency cohort identified from IndiGo's own booking data, with no acquisition spend, since these are people already flying IndiGo repeatedly. The positioning is §43's — "the price you agreed" as an extension of the on-time promise, not as a discount offer. Selling it as a discount would be a mistake: the band's value is certainty, and pricing it as cheapness invites comparison to a fare it is not trying to beat.

## 58. Org Changes Required

One structural change and one new function. The protected-inventory allocation decision sits across revenue management, network planning and commercial product (§30) and cannot be owned by any of them individually — it needs a single accountable owner with authority over all three inputs. And the **Passenger Access function** (§40) must exist before launch, not after: no revenue target, reporting outside commercial, owning SDA-90, the inventory cap and the expiry terms, with a real veto — because a guardrail owned by the team it constrains is not a guardrail.

## 59. Success Criteria

At twelve months from pilot: CBA/1k stable or rising through both a peak and a trough; realisation above 80%, since below that the product is selling options rather than commitments; SDA-90 no worse than baseline on tier-2 and tier-3 routes measured separately. The criterion that actually matters is the fourth: **corridor-level capacity decisions demonstrably made with forward-commitment data as an input.** If the schedule is still being set the way it was before, the product has generated revenue and changed nothing, which is failure dressed as success.

## 60. What Would Change My Mind

If Q2 FY27's ex-fuel, ex-FX CASK growth comes in below Q1's 10.7% on flat capacity, the thesis is wrong and the cost base flexes after all. If the ancillary decline in §25 turns out to be mix — business-heavy traffic with employer-paid bags — rather than anchoring, then §47's top-ranked initiative is worthless and the product diagnosis in §36 is half wrong. If K2 fires and the high-frequency cohort already books early enough to avoid fare volatility, 6E Forward has no buyer. And if domestic traffic returns to growth, the denominator recovers on its own and this becomes a case study about a bad two quarters rather than a structural feature — a real possibility, and management's stated position.

## 62. Limitations

This analysis is built entirely on public disclosure, with no access to IndiGo's cost ledger, trip-frequency distribution, revenue-management parameters or lease terms; the §16 thesis rests on reversing published unit-cost growth against published capacity growth, which is arithmetic on disclosed figures but is not the same as reading the cost base directly. ## 65. Appendices

### Appendix A — Source Conflicts

**A-1. ₹238 Cr vs ₹2,380 Cr net loss — a units error, resolved.** Business Standard reported the Q1 FY27 net loss as "Rs 2,380 crore" against a prior-year profit of "Rs 21,763 crore." IndiGo reports in INR million: the disclosed figures are 2,380 mn (₹238.0 Cr) and 21,763 mn (₹2,176.3 Cr). Both are exactly **10×** the correct rupee-crore figure — the signature of a million-to-crore conversion error, not a disagreement about data. Three independent outlets report ₹238 Cr, and two internal checks confirm it: total income less total expenses gives a loss before tax of ₹238.4 Cr, and the 10× reading would require an FX hit of ₹2,374.4 Cr, **10.22× IndiGo's own disclosed ₹232.4 Cr FX impact on EBITDAR.** ₹238.0 Cr is used throughout; the conflicting source is cited in §64 rather than dropped.

**A-2. Air India group entity figures do not sum to group totals.** ₹51,452 Cr + ₹19,088 Cr = ₹70,540 Cr against a stated ₹71,870 Cr; ₹15,368 Cr + ₹6,767 Cr = ₹22,135 Cr against ₹22,238 Cr — residuals of ₹1,330 Cr and ₹103 Cr, most plausibly consolidation adjustments. Not reconciled; §14 uses the group totals and flags the gap.

**A-3. CASK × ASK does not equal total expenses.** ₹5.71 × 43.5 bn = ₹24,838.5 Cr against ₹25,852.5 Cr — a residual of **₹1,014.0 Cr, 3.92%**, most plausibly net finance cost. IndiGo does not state CASK's expense base. Derived and flagged (§18), not asserted — and it works *against* this file's own argument by understating the RASK–CASK gap.

**A-4. Fleet: "432, down 9" vs "432, up 16."** Both correct on different bases — down 9 sequentially from FY26's closing 441, up 16 on the prior-year quarter — and reconciled by the disclosed movement of 13 inducted against 9 original-order and 13 damp-leased redelivered (§22).

**A-5. CFO identity.** One AI-assisted summarisation of the Q1 FY27 transcript returned an incorrect CFO name; four independent dated sources confirm Kiran Thadimarri, effective 28 July 2026. Logged because it is the kind of error that survives into publication if summaries are trusted over sources (§2).
