# Atomberg — The Metric That Cannot See A Factory

> Atomberg's DRHP leads with a company approaching profitability: adjusted EBITDA loss narrowed 27.78% to ₹37.12 Cr, EBITDA margin improved from −6.35% to −3.86%, ROCE from −24.50% to −22.08%. All true. Net loss went the other way — **wider by 26.49%, to ₹149 Cr** — and the gap between the two grew **68.49%, almost exactly twice the rate revenue grew.** That gap is depreciation and finance cost: the price of becoming a manufacturer. Which is what Atomberg is deliberately becoming, because the thing it won on has stopped being scarce. It built a business on knowing that a BLDC fan uses a third of the electricity of an induction one — private knowledge in 2012, and since 2022 a number the Bureau of Energy Efficiency requires every competitor to print on the box. Crompton's own FY26 disclosure now credits "transition to BLDC technology" for its market-share gains, on 6.26× Atomberg's revenue at a 17.87-point better margin. Atomberg's answer is to move the moat from what it knows to what it owns: proprietary motors, components, and a 50:50 compressor joint venture with Voltas announced the same month as the DRHP. That is a coherent strategy. It is also one that adjusted EBITDA — the metric the IPO is being marketed on — is structurally incapable of seeing.

## 1. Executive Summary

Atomberg filed its DRHP on 21 August 2026: a ₹450 Cr fresh issue plus an offer for sale of 7,65,42,051 shares. FY26 revenue from operations was **₹1,293.77 Cr, up 34.84%**. 🟢

The profitability story is real and improving. Adjusted EBITDA loss narrowed from ₹51.4 Cr to **₹37.12 Cr** (−27.78%); EBITDA margin **−6.35% → −3.86%**; ROCE **−24.50% → −22.08%**; the cost of generating a rupee of revenue fell from **₹1.17 to ₹1.13**. 🟢

Three disclosed facts sit awkwardly beside it.

**The bottom line moved the other way.** Net loss widened **26.49% to ₹149 Cr**. The gap between adjusted EBITDA loss and net loss was ₹66.40 Cr in FY25 and **₹111.88 Cr in FY26 — 68.49% wider, 1.97× the rate revenue grew.** D&A alone is ₹71 Cr, **63.46% of that gap**. 🟢

**The product got slightly worse per rupee.** Materials grew **37.94% against revenue's 34.84%**, taking materials from **55.76% to 57.04% of revenue**. The EBITDA gain is operating leverage — employee cost grew 32.08%, below revenue — not better product economics. 🟢

**And the core decelerated.** Fans are **89.12% of revenue** but supplied only **64.62% of growth**, growing 23.05%. Kitchen appliances, 9.58% of revenue, supplied **31.32%** off a tiny base. 🟢

What ties these together is regulatory. Atomberg's founding insight — a BLDC motor running a fan at roughly a third of an induction fan's draw — was private and unverifiable in 2012. India's mandatory labelling regime changed that: by November 2022 unlabelled fans could not be sold, and the least efficient model permitted drew about 55W against a prior typical 70–75W. 🟢 Efficiency became a printed, comparable number. **Crompton Greaves — FY26 revenue ₹8,096 Cr, 6.26× Atomberg's — now credits its "transition to BLDC technology" for a 60 basis point fan share gain between FY23 and FY26**, at a 15.0% adjusted EBITDA margin against Atomberg's −2.87%. 🟢

Atomberg's response is vertical integration: a components subsidiary, proprietary motors, and a 50:50 compressor JV with Voltas. 🟢 That converts an eroding knowledge advantage into an ownership one — and every rupee of it lands in depreciation and interest, below where the DRHP's headline metric stops.

This case study proposes **Atomberg Proof**: instrument the motor Atomberg already makes, publish third-party-audited *metered* consumption against the *rated* label, and sell that record to buyers who pay the electricity bill at scale. North Star **MSF/1k — Metered Saved kWh per 1,000 installed fan-years**, denominated in installed fan-years so a fan that underperforms counts against it. Guardrail **CMG-90 — Claim-to-Meter Gap at the 90th percentile.** Under a stress test built from Atomberg's own disclosure — components at **1.33% of revenue** — Proof falls from 3rd of 4 to **last**, behind a BEE re-registration programme this case study did not propose.

## 3. Why This Product, Why Now

Three reasons, in order of weight.

**A DRHP is the best document this series can work from.** It is audited, it is filed, and it is written by people with a legal reason not to overstate. Days 52 and 57 both turned on DRHP disclosure. Atomberg filed on 21 August 2026, six days before this was written.

**The strategic question is genuinely unresolved.** Atomberg is the clearest Indian example of a startup that won on a real, physics-based product advantage rather than on discounting or distribution. **And the same month gave us the other half.** The Voltas joint venture was announced 21 August 2026 — the same day as the DRHP. ## 5. Product Overview

Atomberg sells energy-efficient BLDC ceiling fans plus mixer grinders, water purifiers, cold-pressed juicers, smart locks, motors and electronic components. 🟢 The founding product is the fan: a brushless DC motor replacing the induction motor that had been standard in Indian ceiling fans for decades, cutting draw from roughly 70–75W to around a third of that.

The company claims **46.08% market share by cumulative sales value in premium fans** among scaled consumer appliance companies. 🟢 That qualifier — *premium*, among *scaled* companies — is doing real work and §13 treats it accordingly.

---

## 6. History & Timeline

Incorporated **17 April 2012** by Manoj Meena, with Sibabrata Das joining in 2013. The company spent its first years on a product proposition the Indian market had no standardised way to verify: that a fan could use a third of the electricity without performing worse.

Revenue reached **₹959.5 Cr in FY25** and **₹1,293.77 Cr in FY26**. Losses ran alongside throughout — ₹117.8 Cr in FY25, ₹149 Cr in FY26. 🟢 The DRHP followed on **21 August 2026**, the same day as the Voltas JV announcement.

---

## 7. Market Context

The Indian ceiling fan market is large, old and low-margin, historically built on induction motors and price competition among established brands. Its defining recent change is not a new entrant but a regulation: mandatory standards and labelling (§8), which reset the efficiency floor for everyone at once.

No primary-sourced figure for total Indian fan market size or BLDC penetration could be verified to this series' standard, so none is quoted (§13, §63). What can be stated is directional and comes from filings: Crompton grew revenue **50.09% between FY22 and FY26** to ₹8,096 Cr and gained fan share while shifting premium mix from 20% to 27%. 🟢 The category is premiumising, and the incumbents are the ones capturing it.

---

## 8. Regulatory Context

**This is the mechanism the case study turns on.**

India made standards and labelling mandatory for ceiling fans, giving manufacturers until **November 2022** to clear unlabelled stock. 🟢 The effect was immediate: against a then-typical **70–75W** fan, the least efficient model permitted afterwards drew about **55W**, and "super-efficient" meant under **35W**. The new 1-star fan consumed roughly **30% less** than the prior market average, the 5-star roughly **50% less**. As of June 2022, **60% of registered models were 1-star and 25% were 5-star.** 🟢

Read that as a product manager. In 2012, "this fan uses a third of the electricity" was a claim only Atomberg made and no buyer could check. After 2022 it is a mandated label on every competitor's carton, tested to a common protocol. **Regulation did not attack Atomberg. It did something worse: it made Atomberg's argument for it, on everyone's behalf.** At the midpoint of the cited range, the regulated floor alone delivers about **24.14%** saving — **46.67%** of the ~51.72% a super-efficient fan delivers. Nearly half of Atomberg's original pitch is now the legal minimum.

The regime then ratchets: revised star-rating tables run **1 January 2026 to 31 December 2028**, prior-table models valid to **31 December 2025**, registration opening **1 July 2026**. 🟡 Every manufacturer must re-register — and §47 scores that work as the highest-value initiative available, above this case study's own proposal.

## 9. Problem Statement

**For a product organisation:** Atomberg's differentiation was information — a verified efficiency claim in a market that could not verify claims. A labelling regime has since supplied that verification to everyone, and the largest incumbent now uses the same technology to gain share at a margin Atomberg cannot approach. The company's answer is to own the parts rather than the insight, which is capital-intensive, slow, and invisible in the metric it reports on.

**For a buyer:** every fan on the shelf now carries a star rating derived from a laboratory test at a standard condition. Nobody — not the buyer, not the builder, not the manufacturer — knows what any individual fan actually consumed in a real home over a real summer. The label is a promise about a test, not a record of an outcome.

Those are the same gap seen from both sides, and §50 is built in it.

## 10. Who Actually Pays

FY26 revenue from operations of ₹1,293.77 Cr splits as **fans ₹1,153 Cr (89.12%)**, **kitchen appliances ₹124 Cr (9.58%)** and **proprietary components ₹17.2 Cr (1.33%)**. 🟢 Other income adds ₹30 Cr for total income of ₹1,323.77 Cr.

Nine rupees in ten come from a household buying a ceiling fan. The strategy the DRHP describes — components, motors, compressors — is currently **1.33%** of the business, and §47 turns that number into the stress rule.

## 11. User Personas

*Constructed from disclosed operating data and product portfolio, not primary research — see ASSUMPTIONS Part 3.*

**The upgrading urban household** replaces a working induction fan with a BLDC one on an electricity-bill argument, pays a premium, and is the persona Atomberg's 46.08% premium share describes. **The online-first buyer** accounts for the ₹456.45 Cr — **35.28% of revenue** — that comes through marketplaces and the company's own site, and is the only persona Atomberg has a direct relationship with. **The builder or bulk buyer** fits fans by the hundred, pays for none of the electricity, and is therefore indifferent to the entire proposition — which §50 argues is a solvable misalignment rather than a fact of nature.

## 12. User Journey

Discover, compare star ratings and price, buy online or in one of 46,932 retail touchpoints, have it installed, and then never think about it again for a decade.

The last step is the interesting one. A ceiling fan is bought on a claim about future electricity consumption and then generates **no feedback whatsoever** about whether that claim held. There is no moment at which the buyer learns what they actually saved, and therefore no moment at which Atomberg can prove it was right.

## 13. TAM / SAM / SOM — Restricted Form

*Framework note: run in restricted form, sized from the company's own disclosed base rather than a third-party market estimate. Default in this series since Day 57, because no primary-sourced Indian fan market size could be verified (§63).*

The disclosed base is **₹1,293.77 Cr of FY26 revenue** across **46,932 retail touchpoints in about 1,600 cities**, with service reach across **18,000+ pin codes**. 🟢

The claimed position is **46.08% of premium fans by cumulative sales value, among scaled consumer appliance companies.** Three qualifiers narrow that considerably: *premium* excludes the mass segment where most Indian fans are sold; *cumulative sales value* is a stock measure, not a current run-rate, so it credits years of past leadership; and *among scaled consumer appliance companies* excludes an unstated set of competitors. **A 46.08% share on those terms is not a 46.08% share of the fan market**, and the case study does not treat it as one.

The addressable opportunity for §50's proposal is narrower still and better anchored: the builder, institutional and bulk-buyer channel, where a single decision-maker fits hundreds of fans and someone other than that decision-maker pays the electricity bill. Atomberg does not disclose a channel split for it, which is Assumption A4 and the first thing §53 measures.

## 14. Competitive Landscape — Restricted To Filers

*Framework note: restricted to competitors whose numbers are filed. Crompton Greaves Consumer Electricals is listed and reported FY26. **Havells India, Orient Electric and Polycab also file — named rather than silently ignored, but none deep-dived and no estimate constructed.***

**Crompton, FY26: revenue ₹8,096 Cr**, up **50.09%** from FY22. Reported EBITDA margin **10.2%**, adjusted **15.0%**. The ECD segment containing fans is 75% of revenue — about **₹6,072 Cr, 5.27× Atomberg's entire fan business.** Crompton gained **60 basis points** of fan share FY23–FY26, lifted premium mix from **20% to 27%**, and raised revenue per unit **₹547 → ₹759 (+38.76%)**, attributing the gain to its **"transition to BLDC technology."** 🟢

Three things follow, and the second matters most.

**Scale.** Crompton's revenue is **6.26×** Atomberg's at an adjusted EBITDA margin **17.87 points** higher.

**Attribution.** The incumbent is not defending against BLDC — it is *winning with* it, and says so in its own disclosure. Atomberg proved Indian consumers would pay for an efficient fan; Crompton is monetising that finding at six times the volume on distribution built over decades.

**But not costlessly.** Crompton's adjusted margin **fell 1.6 points** over the same window it gained the share (16.6% → 15.0%), and its reported margin sits **4.8 points below** its adjusted one. The transition cost the incumbent margin too — so §16 should not be read as a story about one badly-run company.

## 15. Business Model

Atomberg designs and sells appliances, manufactures increasingly many of the parts inside them, and sells through 626 distributors and direct dealers into 46,932 retail touchpoints plus online marketplaces and its own site. Revenue is unit sales; there is no recurring line, no service contract, no consumables. The company buys materials at 57.04% of revenue, spends 10.43% on advertising and 6.71% on R&D, and loses money.

The model's defining property for this analysis: **everything Atomberg sells is a one-time transaction whose central promise — lower electricity consumption — is realised entirely after the sale, by someone Atomberg never hears from again.**

## 16. Unit Economics

*Framework note: standard operating-leverage decomposition. The work is not computing a margin but noticing that two disclosed profitability measures move in opposite directions.*

| Measure | FY25 | FY26 | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue from operations | ₹959.5 Cr | ₹1,293.77 Cr | **+34.84%** |
| Adjusted EBITDA loss | ₹51.4 Cr | ₹37.12 Cr | **improved 27.78%** |
| EBITDA loss | ₹61 Cr | ₹50 Cr | improved 18.03% |
| EBITDA margin | −6.35% | −3.86% | improved |
| ROCE | −24.50% | −22.08% | improved |
| Cost per ₹1 of revenue | ₹1.17 | ₹1.13 | improved |
| **Net loss** | **₹117.8 Cr** | **₹149 Cr** | **worsened 26.49%** |

Every line improves except the one that counts. 🟢

**The gap is the finding.** Net loss less adjusted EBITDA loss was **₹66.40 Cr** in FY25 and **₹111.88 Cr** in FY26 — **68.49% wider** against revenue growth of 34.84%. **The gap grew 1.97× as fast as the business.** D&A alone is **₹71 Cr, 63.46% of it** and 5.49% of revenue; the remaining **₹40.88 Cr** is finance cost and tax, not separately disclosed (§18, Appendix A-2).

**And the operating improvement is leverage, not product.** Materials grew **37.94%** against revenue's 34.84% — outgrowing it by **3.11 points** — pushing materials from **55.76% to 57.04%** of revenue, while employee cost grew 32.08%, *below* revenue. Fixed costs spread over more revenue; the variable cost of the product itself rose. 🟢

Plainly: **Atomberg is getting better at running a company, slightly worse at making a fan, and spending capital faster than either.**

## 17. Revenue Architecture

Revenue from operations ₹1,293.77 Cr (+34.84%); other income ₹30 Cr; total income ₹1,323.77 Cr. Online contributed **₹456.45 Cr — 35.28%** of revenue, unusually high for an appliance manufacturer and the reason Atomberg has direct customer data at all (§29).

The composition of growth matters more than its size. Fans grew 23.05%, kitchen appliances **6.42×** off a ₹19.3 Cr base, components **4.74×** off ₹3.63 Cr. So the **89.12%** of the business that is fans supplied **64.62%** of growth — a 24.50-point shortfall between its revenue weight and its growth contribution — while kitchen appliances at 9.58% of revenue supplied **31.32%**. 🟢 The core is decelerating relative to the whole, and the diversification is real but small.

## 18. Cost Architecture

FY26 total expenditure **₹1,460 Cr against revenue of ₹1,293.77 Cr** — ₹1.13 spent per rupee earned, improved from ₹1.17. Materials ₹738 Cr (57.04% of revenue), employee benefits ₹210 Cr, advertising and promotion ₹135 Cr (10.43%), labour charges ₹57 Cr, depreciation and amortisation ₹71 Cr. 🟢

**An internal-consistency check worth publishing.** Total income ₹1,323.77 Cr less total expenditure ₹1,460 Cr implies a pre-tax result of **−₹136.23 Cr**, against a reported net loss of **₹149 Cr** — an unexplained **₹12.77 Cr**, most plausibly tax. The equivalent FY25 residual is **₹0.80 Cr**. The FY26 figure is **15.96× the FY25 one**, which is why it is flagged rather than ignored (Appendix A-2). It is derived, not disclosed, and nothing in this case study rests on it.

## 19. The Depreciation Line

₹71 Cr, **5.49% of revenue**, and **63.46%** of the gap between adjusted EBITDA and net loss. For a company operating **two leased facilities** in Pune (Chakan and Chakan-Varale) rather than owned plants, that is a substantial number — it carries right-of-use asset amortisation alongside tooling, moulds and equipment. 🟢

The significance is directional rather than absolute. Depreciation is the accounting shadow of a decision already taken: Atomberg has been buying manufacturing capability for some years, and the Voltas JV commits it to more. Every rupee of that decision appears here and **not** in adjusted EBITDA.

## 20. The Capital Line

The residual **₹40.88 Cr** of the adjusted-EBITDA-to-net-loss gap is finance cost and tax. Atomberg is allocating **₹90 Cr of the ₹450 Cr fresh issue — 20% of it — to repaying or prepaying borrowings**, which is itself a disclosure: the company is carrying debt expensive enough to be worth retiring with IPO proceeds. 🟢

Cash and bank balances stood at **₹41 Cr** against current assets of ₹790 Cr. At FY26's loss rate, ₹41 Cr is **3.30 months** of losses. That is not a distress signal for a company mid-IPO, but it does explain the timing: the fresh issue is **3.02× FY26's net loss**, which is the runway being bought.

## 21. Product Portfolio

Fans, mixer grinders, water purifiers, cold-pressed juicers, smart locks, motors and electronic components. 🟢 Fans are 89.12% of revenue; kitchen appliances 9.58%; components 1.33%.

The portfolio has two logics running at once — horizontal (more appliances for the same household) and vertical (more of the parts inside them). The horizontal bet is working commercially, supplying 31.32% of growth. The vertical bet is, at 1.33% of revenue, still an intention.

## 22. Manufacturing & Vertical Integration

Two **leased** facilities in Pune, a components subsidiary (Atomberg Innovations), proprietary BLDC motors, and a 50:50 joint venture with Voltas for AC compressors with Voltas as anchor customer (§44). 🟢

This is the strategy, and it is defensible: a motor you design and build is a cost advantage and a specification advantage that a competitor buying motors cannot copy quickly. It is also the most capital-hungry route available, it pays back over years, and — the point of §16 — it lands below the line the DRHP leads with. **A company can be right about its strategy and still be measured by a metric that cannot see it.**

## 23. Demand

Revenue grew 34.84% with fans up 23.05%, so demand for the core product is growing well but decelerating relative to the company. No unit volumes are disclosed, so nothing here can be separated into price and volume — a real limitation (§63).

The demand-side fact that matters most is the one in §8: buyers no longer need Atomberg to tell them a fan is efficient, because the label does.

## 24. Pricing

Atomberg prices at a premium and holds 46.08% of premium fans by cumulative sales value. 🟢 Crompton, over FY22–FY26, raised revenue per unit **38.76% from ₹547 to ₹759** and lifted premium mix from 20% to 27%. 🟢

Those two facts describe a squeeze. Atomberg's premium position was built when premium meant *efficient* and only Atomberg was credibly efficient. The incumbent is now climbing into the same price band with the same technology and a cost base that supports a 15.0% margin.

## 25. Distribution

**626 distributors and direct dealers, 46,932 retail touchpoints across about 1,600 cities, service across 18,000+ pin codes** — roughly **75 touchpoints per distributor** and **29 per city**. 🟢

That is a genuinely large network for a fourteen-year-old company and it was expensive to build. It is also, against Crompton's decades of trade relationships, the asset Atomberg is least likely to win on, which is why §47 scores distribution deepening highly on reach but the case study does not propose it.

## 26. Online vs Offline

Online revenue **₹456.45 Cr, 35.28% of the total**. 🟢 For a category still overwhelmingly bought in physical stores, that is the most distinctive structural fact about how Atomberg sells.

It also produces the only asset in this file that Crompton demonstrably lacks: a direct relationship with roughly a third of buyers, and the identity data that comes with it. §50 depends on it.

## 27. Brand & Marketing

Advertising and promotion was **₹135 Cr, 10.43% of revenue**, and the IPO allocates **₹150 Cr to brand awareness and performance marketing** — **1.11× a full year of current spend**, and **1.5× the ₹100 Cr allocated to R&D**. 🟢

That ratio is the most candid line in the DRHP. A company whose founding claim is technological superiority is asking the market for half again as much marketing money as research money. It is the right call if §8 is correct and the technology no longer sells itself — and it is an admission that §8 is correct.

## 28. R&D and Patents

R&D spend **₹86.79 Cr, 6.71% of revenue**, with **254 engineers — 25.32% of the permanent workforce**, implying a permanent headcount of about **1,003**. That is **₹0.34 Cr of R&D per R&D engineer** and revenue of **₹1.29 Cr per permanent employee**. 🟢

A quarter of the company in R&D is a real commitment and unusual for an Indian appliance maker. It is also the resource that makes §50 buildable without new capability: instrumenting a motor you already design is an engineering task, not a moonshot.

## 29. Technology & Data

Atomberg designs its own BLDC motors and controllers, sells 35.28% of its output directly, and ships connected products including smart locks and remote-controlled fans. 🟡

The asset it has and has not exploited: **it controls the motor, so it can measure the motor.** A manufacturer that buys motors from a supplier cannot instrument consumption at source without that supplier's cooperation. This is the single capability Atomberg has that Crompton structurally does not, and the entire proposal in §50 is built on it.

## 30. Org & Leadership

Founders **Manoj Meena** and **Sibabrata Das**, both on the board, neither selling in the offer for sale — a meaningful signal in a listing where seven institutional holders are (§45). 🟢 Investor representation includes A91 Partners' Abhay Kumar Pandey and a nominee director.

The organisational fact §58 turns on: verified-savings measurement would sit across engineering (which owns the motor), marketing (which owns the claim) and sales (which owns the buyer). No existing function owns the gap between what is claimed and what is delivered, which is precisely why the gap is invisible today.

## 31. North Star Metric

**MSF/1k — Metered Saved kWh per 1,000 installed fan-years.**

The denominator is the design choice. It is **installed fan-years**, not units sold — so a fan that ships and then underperforms, sits unused, or is run in a way that saves nothing *lowers* the metric. Selling more fans does not improve MSF/1k unless those fans actually save electricity in the field, for a year, in someone's home.

A saved kilowatt-hour counts only if all four hold, conjunctively: it is **metered, not modelled**; measured against a **like-for-like baseline registered before installation**; **sustained through at least one full summer**; and with **no service intervention** that reset the unit.

Condition 1 is what makes the metric a moat. Every competitor can state a rated efficiency — the star label does it for them. Only a manufacturer that owns the motor can state a *metered* one. Condition 2 is what stops it becoming marketing: a baseline chosen after the fact is a number you can pick.


---
## 32. Supporting Metric Tree

MSF/1k decomposes into four inputs separate teams can own: **instrumentation coverage** (share of shipped units capable of metering), **baseline capture rate** (share of installations with a registered pre-install baseline), **reporting continuity** (share of instrumented units still reporting at twelve months), and **realised saving per fan-year**.

Watch the second and third as a pair. High instrumentation with low baseline capture means Atomberg is measuring consumption it cannot compare to anything — data without a claim. High baseline capture with low continuity means the claim exists but decays before it can be proven, which is worse, because the proposal's whole value is the twelve-month record.

## 33. Jobs To Be Done

*Framework note: stated for the bulk buyer rather than the household, because that is where the proposal's value concentrates and where the current product does the job least well.*

**"When I am fitting three hundred fans into a building whose electricity bill I will not pay, help me justify spending more per unit to someone who will hold me to a number."**

Nothing in the current market does this job. A star rating is a lab result the buyer cannot tie to their own building. The household version of the job — *"help me know whether the fan I paid extra for actually saved me anything"* — has no answer either, and the two are the same missing artefact: a record of what happened after the sale.

---

## 34. Kano Analysis

Efficiency has completed a full Kano migration and that migration is the case study. In 2012 it was an **attractive** attribute — unexpected, delighting, worth a premium. Mandatory labelling made it **must-be**: every fan now has a rating, and lacking a good one is a defect rather than a disadvantage.

Airflow, noise, and remote control are **one-dimensional**. *Verified* saving — a record of what a specific fan actually did — is currently **absent** rather than indifferent: no manufacturer offers it, so no buyer has revealed a preference. Kano cannot classify an attribute nobody has been offered, which argues for testing it cheaply (§54) and against confidence (§47).

---

## 35. Feature Inventory

Fans with BLDC motors and remotes; mixer grinders; water purifiers; cold-pressed juicers; smart locks; motors and electronic components sold as products in their own right; a direct online channel worth ₹456.45 Cr; a service network across 18,000+ pin codes. 🟢

What the inventory does **not** contain, anywhere: any mechanism that reports what a sold product consumed. The company that sells electricity savings has no instrument that measures electricity savings.

---

## 36. Feature Gaps

One gap, stated precisely: **Atomberg makes a claim about the future and keeps no record of the past.** Every unit sold is a promise about consumption, and no unit reports whether the promise held.

Two consequences. Atomberg cannot prove its advantage over a competitor whose star rating matches its own on paper — the label has flattened the comparison and Atomberg has nothing beyond it. And it cannot sell to the buyer for whom the saving is the entire purchase rationale (§33), because that buyer needs evidence and there is none to give.

---

## 37. Switching Costs & Moat

Switching costs are effectively zero. A ceiling fan is a discrete purchase from a shelf; nothing carries over between brands; the star rating makes cross-brand comparison trivially easy by design.

The moat was informational and §8 dissolved it. What remains is real but narrower: proprietary motor design, a components subsidiary, 46,932 touchpoints, and a brand associated with efficiency in the segment where efficiency was first sold. The vertical-integration strategy is an attempt to build a **cost** moat where an **information** moat used to be — sound in principle, and currently **1.33% of revenue**.

## 38. SWOT

**Strengths.** Owns its BLDC motor design and controller; ₹86.79 Cr R&D with 254 engineers (25.32% of headcount); 46.08% of premium fans by cumulative sales value; 35.28% of revenue direct online; 46,932 touchpoints in ~1,600 cities; adjusted EBITDA loss narrowing 27.78%; founders not selling into the IPO.

**Weaknesses.** Net loss widening 26.49% while the headline metric improves; materials cost rising faster than revenue (57.04% of revenue, up 1.28 points); components at 1.33% of revenue after years of investment; ₹41 Cr of cash against a ₹149 Cr annual loss; two leased plants rather than owned; no volume disclosure.

**Opportunities.** BEE re-registration under the revised 2026 tables (§8, and §47's top-scored initiative); the Voltas JV validating compressor technology with an anchor customer; the metered-savings product space, which nobody occupies; kitchen appliances compounding at 6.42×.

**Threats.** Crompton gaining fan share explicitly on BLDC at 6.26× the revenue and a 17.87-point margin advantage; three other listed competitors not analysed here; efficiency as a must-be attribute removing pricing power; capital intensity landing below the reported metric.

## 40. Trust & Safety

**§40 is placed before §50 whenever the proposal creates a harmful incentive. It does here, twice, and the reorder is retained.**

**A fan that reports its run-hours is an occupancy sensor.** Duty cycle over time reveals when a household is home, asleep or away — surveillance data generated as a by-product of an efficiency claim, in a country whose DPDP framework is still being operationalised.

**And measurement becomes marketing unless someone is paid to stop it.** The incentive to choose flattering baselines, favourable climate zones or the best-performing decile is exactly as strong as it looks, and it is how a verification product becomes an advertising product with extra steps.

§51 therefore constrains both: **aggregate energy totals leave the device, not timestamped duty cycles**, and baselines are **registered before installation** and immutable afterwards.

**Guardrail: CMG-90 — Claim-to-Meter Gap at the 90th percentile.** On the worst decile of instrumented installations, the gap between published claim and metered outcome, reported **by climate zone, never in aggregate** (§41). Owned by a **Verification function with no sales or marketing target**, reporting outside commercial, with measurement pipelines **firewalled from marketing by a build-pipeline test**. Two consecutive quarters of breach withdraws the claim for that SKU.


---
## 41. Accessibility & Inclusion

A saving measured in Pune and a saving measured in Chennai are different numbers, and a national average conceals the difference. Reporting CMG-90 in aggregate would let strong results in temperate zones mask weak ones in the hot, humid regions where fans run hardest and where buyers can least afford a claim that does not hold.

CMG-90 is therefore reported **by climate zone**, with a minimum of three zones for a valid pilot (§53) — the same reasoning that put 90th-percentile, disaggregated guardrails on Days 57 through 60.

---

## 42. Sustainability

This is the rare case where the commercial and environmental arguments are the same sentence. A fan that demonstrably saves electricity reduces both a household bill and grid load, and India's fan stock is large enough that the aggregate is not trivial.

The honest addition: **the proposal's environmental value is in the verification, not the fan.** Efficient fans already exist and are already mandated. What does not exist is proof that the efficient fan in a particular building delivered what it promised — which is precisely what would make an energy-efficiency claim financeable rather than merely marketable.

---

## 43. Positioning

Atomberg's position is "the efficient fan," and §8 explains why that is no longer a position — it is a category standard with a government label attached. The ₹150 Cr marketing allocation against ₹100 Cr for R&D (§27) suggests the company knows.

The available repositioning is from *efficient* to *proven*, which is a claim no competitor can currently make and none can quickly copy, because it requires owning the motor. That is what §50 proposes and §43 is the reason it is strategically coherent rather than merely clever.

## 48. Incentive Design

Three incentives are excluded permanently, because each converts the proposal into the harm §40 describes.

**No marketing access to raw per-unit telemetry.** Enforced by the build-pipeline firewall in §40. A marketing function that can query the installation base will find the flattering decile, and it will not be lying when it publishes it.

**No sales incentive tied to instrumented-unit volume.** Compensation on units instrumented produces instrumentation without baselines — measurement that cannot support a claim, which fails North Star condition 2 and wastes the capital.

**No commercial control over baseline definition or climate-zone weighting.** Both are set by the Verification function before launch. These are the two parameters that quietly determine every published number, and whoever controls them controls the result.

## 49. What I Would Not Build

**Not a subscription or connected-fan app.** Nobody wants a relationship with a ceiling fan, and a monthly fee attached to one is a solution looking for a job.

**Not a consumer-facing energy dashboard.** It generates the surveillance data of §40 while addressing the household job (§33) that is worth the least, and it invites exactly the per-unit timestamped collection the mechanics forbid.

**Not a carbon-credit play.** The measurement infrastructure would be similar, the regulatory surface is entirely different, and attaching a credit market to a first product is how a verification instrument becomes an accounting one.

**Not price-matching Crompton.** Atomberg cannot win a margin war against a company with 6.26× the revenue and a 17.87-point margin advantage, and §16 shows the product's variable economics are already deteriorating.

## 50. Feature Proposal — Atomberg Proof

**Atomberg Proof turns the motor Atomberg already designs into an instrument, and turns the resulting record into what it sells to buyers who pay the electricity bill.**

Three parts. The **motor meters itself** — cumulative watt-hours and run-hours, held on-device, reported as periodic aggregate totals rather than timestamped duty cycles (§40). A **baseline is registered before installation**: the fan replaced, its rated draw, the room, the climate zone, immutable once set. And the record is **audited by an independent third party and published per SKU per climate zone** as metered consumption against the rated label — not a marketing claim, a report.

To a household that is reassurance. To a **builder, hotel chain or housing authority** fitting hundreds of fans into buildings whose electricity someone else pays for, it is the missing artefact from §33: evidence that survives procurement scrutiny, turning a per-unit price comparison into a per-kilowatt-hour one.

The case rests on §8, §29 and §35 together: labelling made Atomberg's differentiation a public specification; Atomberg owns the motor and can therefore measure it, which a competitor buying motors cannot do without its supplier; and nothing in the current product set reports what a sold unit consumed. **The proposal is the intersection — the one claim Atomberg can make that Crompton structurally cannot, built from a capability it already has.** It is deliberately narrow: fans only, bulk channel first, three climate zones, killable in two analyst-weeks (§53).


---
## 51. Proposal Mechanics

**What is measured.** Cumulative energy and run-hours at the motor. Not timestamps, not per-hour series — the resolution needed for an annual total and no more (§40).

**What leaves the device.** Periodic aggregate totals only, tied to an installation identifier rather than a person. This is a design parameter set before launch, not a preference to revisit when the data looks commercially interesting.

**The baseline.** Registered at installation and immutable: replaced fan's rated draw, room, climate zone. No baseline, no claim — an installation that skips it is instrumented but does not count toward MSF/1k.

**The audit.** An independent third party verifies the method and samples installations. The published artefact reports metered-versus-rated performance per SKU per zone, **including the underperforming decile** — publishing only the good decile is the failure mode, so the bad one is part of the format.

**The commercial instrument.** For bulk buyers, a procurement contract priced against verified saved kilowatt-hours with a floor, not a per-unit price. For households the report is free and nothing is promised beyond what the audit shows. No consumer app, no timestamped telemetry, no carbon credits (§49).


---
## 52. Proposal Economics

The mechanism is not that Proof raises the price of a fan. It is that it **changes the axis of comparison** in the one channel where Atomberg is currently indistinguishable from a competitor with the same star rating and a better cost base. Against a label, Atomberg loses on price. Against a metered record, the comparison is one only Atomberg can enter.

The secondary effect is on §16's actual problem. Verified savings are the input an energy-efficiency financing or bulk-procurement contract needs, and contracted volume is what justifies capital committed to manufacturing capacity. A vertical-integration strategy financed by unit sales into a commoditising category is what §16 describes; the same strategy financed against contracted, verified demand is a different risk.

**This case study does not model the size of either effect and will not**, because the bulk-channel split Atomberg does not disclose is the first input required (§13, §63). The mechanism is clear, the magnitude is unknown, and §53 buys the magnitude for two analyst-weeks. The downside shape is statable: if adoption lands anywhere near the **1.33%** implied by §47's stress rule, the revenue effect on a ₹1,293.77 Cr base is small — which is a reason to make the test cheap, not to skip it.

---

## 53. Phase 0 — Two Analyst-Weeks, With Kill Criteria

Phase 0 builds no product. It uses warranty registrations, the direct online channel's order data (35.28% of revenue), and a bench test of existing motors.

**K1 — the metered saving does not match the rated one.** Bench-test and field-sample existing units against their rated draw. If metered consumption exceeds rated by more than **20%** across the sample, Proof would publish a record that damages Atomberg rather than differentiating it, and the correct response is engineering, not marketing.

**K2 — named as the criterion most likely to fire.** If the bulk and institutional channel is too small a share of Atomberg's revenue to matter, the proposal's value concentrates in a segment that cannot move the P&L. Atomberg does not disclose this split; it is Assumption A4; and it is the number that decides whether any of this is worth building. **Bet on this one.**

**K3 — baselines cannot be captured at installation.** Fans are installed by electricians the company does not employ. If fewer than half of a pilot's installations return a usable pre-install baseline, condition 2 of the North Star cannot be met at scale and the metric is unmeasurable. Threshold: a valid pilot needs **2,000 installations across at least 3 climate zones**.

Any of the three firing stops the work.

---

## 54. Experiment Design & Falsification

**Arm A — control.** Current product, star rating, existing bulk sales motion.

**Arm B — the cheap imitation, and the real test.** A **third-party-audited claim with no per-unit metering**: an independent lab tests a sample and publishes a report, branded by Atomberg. Deliverable in weeks, nearly free, and it supplies *credibility* without the *instrumentation* that is Proof's entire justification. **If Arm B moves bulk conversion as much as Arm C, the metering is theatre — ship Arm B and stop.**

**Arm C — full Atomberg Proof:** metered motors, registered baselines, audited per-zone publication, savings-linked procurement contracts.

**Pre-registered decision rule R1.** Arm C proceeds only if it beats Arm B by more than **10 percentage points on MSF/1k-linked bulk conversion across two procurement cycles including one full summer**, with **CMG-90 no worse than baseline in every climate zone measured separately** (§41). The threshold is high deliberately: Arm C costs roughly an order of magnitude more than Arm B (§47).

**What would falsify the thesis rather than the proposal.** §16 claims capital intensity is outrunning the business. That is falsified — cheaply, by Atomberg itself — if FY27's adjusted-EBITDA-to-net-loss gap grows more slowly than revenue. No experiment required.

## 56. Roadmap

Phase 0 is two analyst-weeks on data the company already holds (§53). If it survives, a pilot of 2,000 installations across at least three climate zones through one full summer, bulk channel only — because that is where the job is worth most (§33) and where a baseline can actually be captured at installation. If R1 clears, extension to the institutional catalogue.

Consumer rollout is deliberately **not** on this roadmap and should not be until CMG-90 has held through a full summer in every zone. A verification product that publishes a claim it later has to withdraw is worse than no verification product.

## 57. Go-To-Market

The bulk and institutional channel is reached through the 626 distributors and direct dealers who already serve project business, plus direct enterprise sales for the largest accounts — not through the 46,932 retail touchpoints, which are built for a different buyer.

The positioning is §43's: from *efficient* to *proven*. That must not be sold as a discount or a feature. The artefact is a report, and the pitch is that Atomberg is the only manufacturer that can produce one — which stops being true the moment a competitor starts instrumenting motors, so speed matters more than polish.

---

## 58. Org Changes Required

One new function, and it must exist before the first published claim, not after. The **Verification function** (§40) owns CMG-90, the baseline definition, the climate-zone weighting and the audit relationship; it carries no sales or marketing target and reports outside commercial; and it holds a real veto over publication.

The structural point from §30: today the gap between what Atomberg claims and what it delivers is owned by nobody, which is why it is invisible. Naming an owner is most of the change.

## 59. Success Criteria

At twelve months from pilot: MSF/1k rising through a full summer; baseline capture above 50% of instrumented installations, since below that the metric cannot be computed honestly; reporting continuity above 80% at twelve months; CMG-90 no worse than baseline in every zone measured separately.

The criterion that actually matters is the fifth: **at least one bulk procurement contract priced against verified saved kilowatt-hours rather than per-unit price.** Without that, Proof has produced an interesting dataset and changed nothing about how Atomberg sells — which is failure wearing the costume of success.

## 60. What Would Change My Mind

If FY27's adjusted-EBITDA-to-net-loss gap grows more slowly than revenue, the §16 thesis is wrong and the capital intensity is being absorbed.

If materials cost falls back below 55.76% of revenue, the product-economics finding was a one-year sourcing effect, not erosion.

If K2 fires and the bulk channel is immaterial to Atomberg's revenue, Proof has no buyer worth serving and should not be built.

If Crompton's fan share gains stall while its margin keeps falling, then §14's reading is wrong: the incumbent would be buying share at a price it cannot sustain, and Atomberg's position is stronger than this file credits.

And if the Voltas JV turns out to be capital-light — a licensing or technology arrangement rather than a plant Atomberg part-funds — then §22's characterisation of the pivot as capital-hungry is overstated.
